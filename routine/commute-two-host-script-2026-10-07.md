## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

SAM: Our deep dive today isn't about markets at all. It's a story out of Siberia, and our source is The Economist, whose newsletter this week carried the piece "Infectious rumours: the plague may have escaped from a lab in Siberia," with the subhead that Russian secrecy means it is unclear how grave the threat is. We only have the newsletter blurb, so everything beyond that thesis comes from open reporting, and we'll flag what's solid versus disputed.
ALEX: Start with the facts as reported. A 28-year-old laboratory technician, Darya Shipilova, worked at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Institute. According to Russian independent outlets, including Novaya Gazeta and Meduza, she was exposed around September 25, allegedly when a test tube containing pneumonic plague broke. She was hospitalized on September 29, placed on a ventilator, and died overnight on October 1 into the 2nd. Reports differ slightly on the dates.
SAM: Pneumonic plague being the airborne form, the one that terrifies epidemiologists.
ALEX: Right. Untreated it's close to uniformly fatal, but it responds to antibiotics if caught early, which is why the contact tracing matters. Reports say about 189 contacts are under observation, more than 100 reportedly in hospitals, and at least one hospital was quarantined.
SAM: And the Russian government's account?
ALEX: Rospotrebnadzor, the health regulator, denies any accident and says she died of pneumonia of unknown origin. Independent Russian media say it was plague. The White House says it's monitoring, and Forbes reported over the weekend that Washington is pressing Moscow for disclosure.
SAM: So what's the actual story? One death in a country of 140 million isn't a pandemic.
ALEX: Correct, and that's the Economist's point. The death is small. The information vacuum is the story. Moscow's non-answer is itself the signal, because of the precedent. In April 1979 an anthrax leak from a military facility in Sverdlovsk killed dozens of people. The Soviet government blamed contaminated meat for 13 years, until Boris Yeltsin finally admitted in 1992 that it was the lab.
SAM: And there's the context that makes the reflex worse. Russia's anti-plague institutes are legacy Soviet institutions that hold live Yersinia pestis for surveillance and vaccine work.
ALEX: Which is legitimate work, and also hard to tell apart from weapons work from the outside. Biopreparat, the Soviet bioweapons program, worked on antibiotic-resistant plague, according to defectors like Vladimir Pasechnik. And the Biological Weapons Convention has no verification mechanism at all. After an accident, voluntary transparency is the only check the world has.
SAM: Let's give the other side. What's contested?
ALEX: A lot. Whether it was plague at all, since the primary evidence is independent reporting and not an official lab result. Whether it was an accident or an unrelated infection, because Siberia has endemic natural plague foci. Whether quarantining nearly 200 people is proportionate caution or theater. The one thing that would settle it is genome sequencing, to tell a natural strain from a lab strain, and Russia hasn't shared any data.
SAM: There's an implicit question about whether the secrecy would be rational for Moscow even if the lab is clean.
ALEX: That's the game theory. A government that has lied before can't buy credibility cheaply, so a truthful denial and a false denial sound identical, and the world has to price the worst case. The cost of that is borne by everyone, not just Russia. Disclosure that comes late arrives all at once, and that's true of a pathogen and of a bad infrastructure deal.
SAM: And for a listener like ours, the practical takeaway?
ALEX: Don't take a position on the facts yet. This is fast-moving. Watch for three things: a WHO statement, any sequencing data, and any second case outside the original cluster. Absent those, this stays a story about trust, not about a pathogen.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

SAM: Mandarin vocab, and today both words are connective, argument-building language from the world of trade talks. The first is 以至于, yǐzhìyú.
ALEX: 以至于 links a cause to an extreme result: "so much so that." It's neutral-to-written register, common in news and analysis. The key contrast is with 以致, which also introduces a result but usually a negative one. 以至于 is more neutral, so it can describe a result that's surprising without being bad. Here's the pattern, with a long condition first, then the outcome. 价格战打得太久，以至于企业的利润几乎消失了。(The price war has gone on so long that corporate profits have nearly vanished.)
SAM: And you'd use it after a degree or duration expression, like 太, 那么, or 很久.
ALEX: Exactly. Another example. 房地产低迷持续太久，以至于居民宁可存钱也不愿消费。(The property slump has lasted so long that households would rather save than spend.)
SAM: Our second word is 取决于, qǔjuéyú, "to depend on."
ALEX: 取决于 is an abstract, neutral-to-formal collocation. The thing that decides the outcome comes after it. Its colloquial near-synonym is 看…而定, which sounds more casual, like "it depends on." 取决于 is what you'd see in a financial article. 谈判能否取得进展，取决于双方是否愿意让步。(Whether the talks make progress depends on whether both sides are willing to give ground.)
SAM: And it pairs naturally with 能否 or 是否, an "or not" structure.
ALEX: Yes. Something like 消费能否回暖，取决于房价是否止跌。(Whether consumption recovers depends on whether home prices stop falling.) Don't confuse it with 决定, which is a verb that takes the deciding thing as the subject, so the grammar runs the other way. Hear both of them in the China segment next.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY

SAM: Op-eds. Our pick is a theme that has been all over the commentary pages this week: the bond selloff, and whether it's a regime change. Mohamed El-Erian, the economist and Allianz adviser, argues this is "no ordinary bond-market sell-off" and that, if selling continues, it could mark the beginning of a structural economic shift, more enduring and more globally consequential than most previous episodes of market volatility.
ALEX: Steelman first, because it's a serious argument. El-Erian points to four forces. Massive AI-driven corporate borrowing is competing with the Treasury for the same dollars. Traditional foreign buyers are retreating, China for geopolitical reasons while Japan and the Gulf states deal with problems at home. Real yields have surged. And the stress is spilling into allied sovereign markets. His conclusion is that the mechanisms that used to tame rising rates, such as foreign central banks buying and the Fed stepping in, won't work as efficiently now.
SAM: The counter-view is out there too. Richard Baker, writing in a business-journal column, says the current yields don't rattle him, and that rates are finally returning to where they should be after the near-zero era that followed 2008. He reads higher yields as the unwinding of abnormal conditions, and the market as functioning properly.
ALEX: My counter-take sits between them. Baker is right about the level, and wrong about the speed. A 30-year at 5.64% isn't irrational against a 4% historical average, but a move this fast hits balance sheets that were built for the old regime, and the stress shows up in the places we've covered all week, utilities issuing convertibles and small caps lagging. El-Erian is right about the buyers and may overstate the permanence. The retreat of foreign demand is a real fact, but a yield high enough does attract domestic buyers, as Goldman has noted about 30-year paper above 5.5%.
SAM: So who's right?
ALEX: The test is whether the market clears without help. If the 10-year holds at 5.27% without a policy response, Baker's normalization story looks better. If Treasury or the Fed has to lean in, El-Erian's regime-change story does. Watch the next long-bond auction.
