## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

SAM: Today's Deep Dive is a change of ground. We have spent a lot of this month on power, tariffs and capex, so we are going to talk about how people decide whether a machine has a mind. The piece is a study from LMU Munich published in the journal Cognition, led by Louis Longin with Ophelia Deroy and Bahador Bahrami as senior authors, and we should be upfront that we found it through the university's press release and its reprints rather than the full paper.
ALEX: The design is straightforward. Nearly 1,100 participants read short scenarios in which an agent, either an AI system or a human, responded to its environment, and then rated how aware or conscious that agent was. The researchers varied how responsive the agent was.
SAM: The headline finding is that responsiveness raised the ratings for both, but a gap remained. Even the most responsive AI was judged less conscious than the least responsive human. Behaviour that was identical did not buy equal credit.
ALEX: The wrinkle is in the vocabulary. When the question used a more neutral word like awareness, the distinction largely shrank, and people were willing to say an AI is aware in the sense of tracking and responding. Bahrami's point, as the press release relays it, is that people draw the line at consciousness, the felt, inner part.
SAM: So the public is making a two-tier judgment. A machine can perceive and respond, but it does not get the full inner-life status however well it performs.
ALEX: Here is where I would push. A study like this measures attribution, not truth. It tells you what ordinary people will believe and accept, and it explicitly does not establish whether any AI system experiences anything. Those are 2 different questions, and the risk is that a firm intuition gets mistaken for evidence.
SAM: But the attribution question is the one that drives policy. If people consistently refuse to grant moral status to a system that behaves just like a person, then any rule built on perceived sentience, whether welfare protections for models or limits on emotional-companion products, runs into a public that has already decided the answer.
ALEX: There is also a design implication for products. Companion apps are explicitly built to close the behavioural gap, to make the agent responsive, warm and persistent. The study suggests that closing the behavioural gap is not enough to close the status gap, which could mean those products hit a ceiling of trust, or could mean the ceiling moves as people get used to them.
SAM: That second point is the real open question, and the study is a snapshot. Attitudes toward a thing you have only read a vignette about are not the same as attitudes toward a voice you have talked to every day for a year. Familiarity changed how people treated pets, and it may do the same here.
ALEX: And the counter-argument from the philosophy side is that the intuition may be tracking something real, like biology, a body, stakes. Researchers in that camp would say the participants are right for the wrong reason, and that the gap should persist even if behaviour becomes perfect.
SAM: Either way, the useful takeaway is narrow and solid. Judgments of awareness and judgments of consciousness come apart in the public mind, and anyone designing or regulating around one should check which word they are actually measuring. Next time someone says the public thinks the machine is sentient, ask which question was asked.
ALEX: Source path for the record: this was found through web research, a university press release on a peer-reviewed study, not a newsletter or a viral thread.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES

SAM: The Department of Energy issued Order 202-26-50 under Section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act, covering PJM from October 13th through the 16th. In plain terms it gives PJM a tool it has not had in this form: as a last resort, before or during a level-3 emergency, it can direct backup generation, standby units and battery storage at large-load sites, defined as 50 megawatts or more at a delivery point.
ALEX: The order also lets PJM tell named generating units to run, which is the more conventional 202(c) use. Reporting varies on whether it was issued October 12th or 13th and on how many states it covers, so check the order text, but the load-site backup piece is what is new.
SAM: The context is a tight autumn. PJM forecast a peak of about 111,500 megawatts on Tuesday, a reserve margin within 900 megawatts on Wednesday, and 61,591 megawatts of planned maintenance outages. That is a shoulder-season problem rather than a summer-peak problem.
ALEX: And that is the part I find most interesting. The earlier 202(c) orders in May and July did not result in any call on large-load backup, and we have no evidence yet that anyone has been dispatched this time. So this is a legal option being kept open, not an event. But the architecture is the story. Maintenance season plus load growth leaves the margin thin on days nobody built a model for.
SAM: What does that do to the data-center developer?
ALEX: It turns flexibility from a slide-deck promise into a contract term. If the grid operator can reach behind the meter and call your backup generation, then your onsite gas gensets and batteries are no longer private insurance. They are part of the system, and counterparties will ask for them as a condition of interconnection. That raises the capital cost per megawatt of firm service, and it makes the dispatchable backup a thing you have to permit, fuel and test as seriously as the main plant.
SAM: There is also an emissions angle, because those backup units were permitted on the assumption of very few run hours.
ALEX: Right, and the air permits are written around that assumption. If dispatch becomes routine, the permit limits become the binding constraint before the equipment does. That is exactly the kind of second-order bottleneck that does not show up in the interconnection queue count.
SAM: Second item, and a big one for the equipment side. Enerflex disclosed in a filing on October 13th an award to design, build and assemble more than 1 gigawatt of behind-the-meter gas generation for an unnamed North American data-center developer. That follows about 450 megawatts announced October 1st, so its behind-the-meter backlog is now above 1.75 gigawatts, with deliveries running through 2028.
ALEX: It also authorised about $100 million to expand its Engineered Systems capacity, of which $15 million falls inside this year's capex guidance. The customer and whether the sites are fully off-grid are not disclosed.
SAM: Why does that matter beyond the headline gigawatt?
ALEX: Because it is the packager, not the turbine maker, being paid to expand. The scarce thing has moved down the chain. Everyone talks about turbine slots, and the order book says the assembly, balance-of-plant and skid-integration capacity is also a real constraint. And with deliveries in 2028, these are bridge-power plays. The developer is buying time against a grid that cannot connect it, and it is paying a premium to get it.
SAM: Those 2 stories are really one story seen from both ends. The grid operator wants to reach into private generation in an emergency, and the developers are building more private generation so they do not have to wait for the grid.
ALEX: Exactly. The tension is that the same megawatts cannot be both the developer's independent supply and the operator's emergency reserve. At some point a rule has to say which one wins when they collide, and the first emergency that actually tests it will set the precedent.
SAM: One more caution on a third item. Oracle has reportedly moved to protect itself financially against delay or cancellation of a 2.5 gigawatt campus in Doña Ana County, New Mexico, after state regulators blocked its gas pipeline. We did not confirm the date of that report, so we are flagging it as a data point rather than news.
ALEX: But the logic is consistent. The gate on that site is gas supply permitting, not interconnection. The binding constraint depends on the project, which is why generic queue statistics keep missing the point.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

SAM: Today's 2 Mandarin words come from this morning's China trade data. The first is 压力, yālì, pressure. Alex, what is the register?
ALEX: It is neutral and extremely common, and it is more flexible than it looks. It works for physical pressure, but the finance and policy usage is the abstract one: 面临压力, to face pressure, 施加压力, to apply pressure, 压力越来越大, the pressure keeps growing. A 书面语 version of the same idea would be 压力 together with a formal verb like 承受 or 缓解.
SAM: Near-synonym?
ALEX: 压迫 is the contrast. 压迫 means oppression or physical compression, and it is heavier and more negative. 压力 is the neutral measure of strain, and it can come from a person, a policy or a market. Here is an example. 欧盟要求中国平衡贸易，中国面临的压力越来越大。(The EU is asking China to rebalance trade, and the pressure on China keeps growing.)
SAM: Second word, 依然, yīrán, still, as before. It looks simple, so what is the catch?
ALEX: The catch is register. 依然 is 书面语, the written and formal cousin of 还是 and 仍然. In a news report you read 局势依然紧张, the situation remains tense, while in conversation you would say 还是很紧张. It signals continuity against an expectation of change.
SAM: And the collocation?
ALEX: It sits before the verb or adjective, usually after the subject, and it often follows a concession clause, as in 尽管…依然…. Here is an example. 出口增长很快，但国内需求依然偏弱。(Exports are growing fast, but domestic demand is still on the weak side.)
SAM: Contrast with 仍然?
ALEX: 仍然 is nearly the same and just as formal, with a slightly firmer flavour of persisting despite something. 依然 can feel a little more descriptive, a state that has simply carried on. Another example. 压力依然存在。(The pressure still exists.)
SAM: Note that 依然 describes a situation that continues. It is not used for a repeated action, where you would use 又 or 再.
ALEX: Right. We will hear both words in the China segment next.

## SEGMENT NINE — ARTS & CULTURE

SAM: Starting with visual art. The Philadelphia Museum of Art opened its Marcel Duchamp retrospective on October 10th, running to January 31st, 2027. It has more than 300 works, and it is the first major American retrospective in over 50 years. It was organised with MoMA and the Centre Pompidou, and the Philadelphia museum holds the world's largest Duchamp collection.
ALEX: The question with a show like this is whether you can exhibit a man who spent his life refusing categories. WHYY's review says the exhibition tries to contain an artist who refused to be contained. The MoMA version drew more than 611,000 visitors, but its reviews were split. Frieze said it was built like a mausoleum and that Duchamp vanishes into the shadow of his own legend, while ARTnews said it reintroduces him rather than upends him.
SAM: That tension is Duchamp's own joke. The readymade, the urinal, the bicycle wheel, only works because a museum declares it art, and a 300-work survey with a catalogue is the museum's final victory over the gesture. So the show is always going to be a little bit of a trap, and the best version is the one that lets you feel that.
ALEX: Philadelphia has an advantage, because it owns the Large Glass and the Étant donnés, the peepshow installation you look at through a hole in a door. Those are works that cannot travel, and they change what a retrospective can say about the late work.
SAM: Second item, a preview. The Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam opens Whistler: Dandy and Disruptor on Friday, October 16th, running to January 10th, 2027. We could not find reviews yet, so this is a flag, not a verdict.
ALEX: The pairing is interesting regardless. Whistler was a showman and a litigant, and his argument that a painting is an arrangement of tone and not a story, the famous Nocturne and Symphony titles, is exactly the opposite of the earnest Van Gogh. A museum devoted to the earnest painter is hosting the great dandy of the aesthetic movement.
SAM: And for literature, the Booker Prize winner will be named on November 9th at Old Billingsgate in London, for the £50,000 prize. The shortlist includes M. John Harrison, Marlon James, Luke Kennard, Rebecca Perry, Elizabeth Strout and Douglas Stuart.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY

SAM: First, Yan Wu in the Washington Post, writing on October 13th. The argument is that AI has not caused the jobpocalypse people predicted, but it will force Gen Z to rethink what work means.
ALEX: The steelman is fair. Aggregate unemployment has not exploded, young people are adapting, and the loudest forecasts of mass layoffs have not arrived. If you want to give a young person advice, telling them to build skills that complement the tools is not unreasonable.
SAM: The counter-take is that the damage does not show up as layoffs. It shows up as hiring that never happens. If firms simply stop opening entry-level roles, the layoff statistics stay clean while a generation loses the first rung of the ladder. And last month's payrolls gain was only 29,000.
ALEX: There is also a quiet shift of responsibility inside the phrase rethink work. It asks the individual to absorb an adjustment that is better handled by employers and policy. A 22-year-old cannot rethink a hiring freeze. So the column is right that the apocalypse has not come, and the wrong lesson is to conclude that nothing is wrong.
SAM: Second piece, a backup of sorts. Leana Wen, also in the Washington Post, argues that SNAP should not cover soda, pointing to studies of restriction programmes.
ALEX: The steelman is that a nutrition programme should not subsidise the single largest source of added sugar in the diet, and that the evidence shows purchases shift. It is a modest, targeted intervention rather than a ban.
SAM: The counter is that the evidence is mostly about purchase shifts, not health outcomes, and the administrative burden and stigma fall on the people in the programme. A rule that makes a checkout line a place of surveillance has costs the studies do not count. And if you worry about sugar, the tax is a cleaner instrument than a restriction on the poorest shoppers.
ALEX: Which is a decent illustration of the difference between an argument that is correct on the narrow question and one that is wise as policy. Both are worth reading.
