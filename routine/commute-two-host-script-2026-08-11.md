## SEGMENT — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Alright, Deep Dive. This one's dense, but it's genuinely one of the sharper technical arguments we've read in a while — a SemiAnalysis feature that landed in the inbox yesterday.
ALEX: The piece is about a piece of software called TileRT, and the claim is that it closes most of the interactivity gap between ordinary Nvidia GPUs and the purpose-built low-latency chips — Cerebras, Groq's LPU, SambaNova.
SAM: Walk me through why that gap exists in the first place.
ALEX: So the roofline math — the theoretical ceiling based purely on memory bandwidth — says an 8-GPU B200 node, with 64 terabytes a second of HBM bandwidth, should be capable of about 3,047 tokens per second per user running a model like GLM-5 at batch size one.
SAM: And real-world engines don't get anywhere close.
ALEX: Nowhere close. And the reason isn't bandwidth — bandwidth has actually grown 2 to 3 times per hardware generation. The bottleneck is kernel-launch latency. Every step of generating a token involves thousands of sequential kernel launches, and that overhead hasn't moved even as the hardware got faster.
SAM: So TileRT's fix is?
ALEX: A single, statically-compiled "persistent engine kernel" that stays resident on the GPU for the entire decode lifecycle, instead of launching thousands of separate kernels per token. Software, not silicon.
SAM: Give me the actual numbers, because "closes most of the gap" is a big claim.
ALEX: On a single B200 8-GPU node: 340 tokens per second per user at an 8,000-token input, 1,000-token output setup — that's 1.9 times the prior best result, which was a GB300 NVL72 system at 181.4. At a shorter 1,000-in, 1,000-out FP8 setup, TileRT hits 494.2 tokens per second per user — 3.6 times the previous best conventional FP8 engine.
SAM: And end-to-end latency?
ALEX: Beats the prior-best GLM-5.1 setup by 4.5 times at that same 1,000-in, 1,000-out configuration. And on an iso-cost basis — same dollar spend — TileRT runs roughly twice as interactive as a traditional engine.
SAM: There's got to be a catch.
ALEX: There is — it only works at batch size one. Throughput comes in at 160.4 tokens per GPU, versus a GB300's 240 tokens per GPU when you're running 12 concurrent requests. SemiAnalysis calls it "a single-passenger rocketship" — blazing fast for one user, but you sacrifice the multi-tenant efficiency that makes serving cheap at scale.
SAM: Is anyone actually running this in production, or is it a benchmark exercise?
ALEX: Two real deployments already — Xiaomi's MiMo V2.5 Pro UltraSpeed tier, and Z.ai's GLM-5.1 HighSpeed tier. Both use a split architecture — vLLM handles the prefill stage, TileRT handles decode — and critically, neither provider bought any new hardware to do it.
SAM: Which is the whole argument, isn't it — that fungibility beats purity.
ALEX: That's the author's actual thesis. A GPU-fleet operator can carve out a premium "speed tier" from capacity they already own, and reallocate the prefill-to-decode ratio just by changing a config file. An ASIC fleet locks in a fixed speed-to-capacity ratio the day you buy it, and re-racking it to change that ratio takes months.
SAM: So this directly squeezes Cerebras, Groq, and SambaNova's addressable market.
ALEX: From below, yes — though the piece is careful to say the SRAM-based roofline those chips run on still wins outright on raw ceiling, and on small dense models specifically. This isn't "ASICs are dead." It's "the GPU fleet you already have can now compete with them on the metric that used to be their whole reason to exist."
SAM: A software layer eating a hardware moat. That's the throughline of the day in miniature, honestly — speed and flexibility beating a fixed, purpose-built bet.
ALEX: There's a real business question buried in here too — this is already live in production at Xiaomi and Z.ai, which means the "speed tier" pricing model is going to spread fast. If you can sell a premium interactivity tier off hardware you already own, with zero incremental capex, that's close to pure margin.
SAM: Which raises the question of whether Cerebras and Groq need to cut prices just to defend their reason for existing, given a chunk of their pitch was "GPUs simply can't do this."
ALEX: That's exactly the pressure the piece flags. It doesn't kill the ASIC case outright — small dense models and the raw ceiling still favor purpose-built silicon — but it meaningfully narrows the range of workloads where "we need dedicated hardware" is actually true.
