## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Our deep dive this morning comes from The Economist Today, which landed in your inbox on Friday with the headline: Our analysis of the leaked Russian death toll in Ukraine. The blurb is one line, that hackers have released records they claim come from Russia's statistics agency. We've rebuilt the rest from the open reporting.
ALEX: Here's what happened. Between October 29 and November 1, Rosstat, Russia's federal statistics service, said it was hit by coordinated cyberattacks. It insists its protected databases were never compromised and nothing leaked. About a week later, two groups, the Belarusian Cyber Partisans and a pro-Ukrainian outfit called Silent Crow, began publishing what they say is around 23 terabytes of Rosstat data, including the civil registry of deaths.
SAM: And the number in those records is very large. Counting only death certificates where the cause is coded as military action or its consequences, the Cyber Partisans get a floor of 393,881 Russian deaths from February 2022 to August 27 of this year. Bloomberg ran its own pass over the data and got about 392,700, which is close.
ALEX: The ceiling is higher. Silent Crow adds non-combat military deaths and certificates with no cause listed, and reaches about 450,100. The Financial Times found roughly 12,000 more probable military deaths among men who died at or near the front, and once you allow for late paperwork, the FT projects something like 550,000 through August.
SAM: Compare that with what we thought we knew. Mediazona and the BBC Russian service, doing it the hard way from obituaries and local reports, had named 285,881 dead. Their statistical estimate from probate registries was around 352,000 by the end of 2025. The Economist's own model, published October 30, put the range at 374,000 to 770,000.
ALEX: So the leak lands inside the honest estimates, at the high end of the name counts and the low end of the model. If it's genuine, the open-source community was undercounting by somewhere between a quarter and a half. And the Moscow Times, which checked a random sample of 50 names against open-source lists and found the metadata consistent with a genuine database export, says the war is now the fourth most common cause of death in Russia since 2022, and the rate is accelerating.
SAM: The most revealing figures are regional. In Buryatia, Tuva and Zabaykalsky, about 1 in 3 deaths of men aged 18 to 49 were war-related through mid-2024. In Moscow and St Petersburg it's about 1 in 10. Al Jazeera adds the foreign recruits: Kenyans offered about 2,715 dollars a month plus bonuses up to 9,309 dollars, more than 1,000 recruited, and by July casualties were reportedly outpacing recruitment.
ALEX: Now the caveats, and they're serious. Nobody has independently verified the leak. The hackers are combatant-aligned, and both groups have every reason to inflate. The Cyber Partisans themselves concede the military-action code may sweep in civilians and deaths from other conflicts, and one sceptical analyst claims a query for Bucha returned Ukrainian citizens. Registration lags also mean 2026 is undercounted. The Kremlin hasn't specifically denied this; its line since January has been that only Defence Ministry figures count.
SAM: So why does it matter if it can't be verified? I'd argue the regional skew is the most important finding, because it explains the domestic quiet. The war's cost is being paid in the poor periphery and by foreign contract soldiers, while the two cities that make Russian politics lose 1 in 10. That isn't a political system under strain. It's one that has outsourced the dying.
ALEX: And the second-order read for the peace talks is uncomfortable. The usual Western assumption is that enough losses eventually force Moscow to the table. These records say the Kremlin has known the true number all along, something like 400,000 or more, and has kept paying it. If the state's own ledger didn't change its calculus, an unverified leak of that ledger won't either.
SAM: Russia's statistics service has just become a security liability. Expect Moscow to classify more, publish less and delay the demographic releases that analysts have used as a back door into war losses. The Economist's model exists partly because the official data was already thin, and that window is now closing.
ALEX: So the leak may be the most accurate count we ever get, because it probably ends the data series it came from.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: To energy, and from your inbox, CTVC's Sunday edition and the week's DealFlow. The headline deal: NRG is selling 6 gas plants, 4.9 gigawatts, back to LS Power for 4 billion dollars in cash.
ALEX: Back to LS Power is the strange part, because NRG bought these plants from LS Power less than a year ago. The list is Ravenswood in New York City, Ocean State in Rhode Island, Wallingford in Connecticut, and Jack County, Johnson County and R.W. Miller in Texas. That works out to roughly 816 dollars a kilowatt. Closing is targeted for the second quarter of 2027, pending Hart-Scott-Rodino, a FERC Section 203 approval and the New York PSC.
SAM: NRG puts the money into 2 billion dollars of additional debt paydown and 1 billion of extra buybacks, keeps about 20 gigawatts, half of it in ERCOT, and says its 1.2-gigawatt bring-your-own-power data-center project is unaffected.
ALEX: The read is that NRG is selling merchant gas in New York and New England and keeping its position in Texas, which is where the data-center load is and where it can sign bilateral deals. For the rest of the market, the number to notice is the price. Infrastructure money is still paying about 800 dollars a kilowatt for existing combined-cycle and peaking fleets when new-build gas costs well north of 2,000. As long as that gap holds, buying existing plants beats building new ones, and LS Power goes from about 2.2 gigawatts of gas to 11.5 once its Constellation acquisition closes as well.
SAM: Second, a write-down. Vistra's 10-Q books about 269 million dollars of partial impairment on Moss Landing's 350-megawatt battery, the phase that wasn't destroyed in the January 2025 fire. Net book value is now about 37 million dollars, and Vistra says whether it restarts at all is uncertain.
ALEX: That means all 3 batteries on the site have now been written down, after roughly 400 million on the 300-megawatt phase and 155 million on the 100-megawatt phase. Vistra has closed its fire investigation. The lesson the industry is drawing is about design: NMC cells packed indoors in one building turned into a stranded asset. Expect insurers and lenders to require LFP chemistry, containerised layouts and real spacing on any battery project near a data center, and to charge for it when they don't get it.
SAM: Third, a storage failure of a different kind. ESS Tech, the iron-flow long-duration storage company, filed for Chapter 7 liquidation in Delaware on November 13. It has stopped operating, every employee has been let go, and a trustee now controls the assets.
ALEX: So any buyer holding ESS warranties is now an unsecured creditor. For the rest of long-duration storage, counterparty risk is now priced in. Expect procurement teams to demand insurance wraps on warranties and escrowed performance guarantees from the surviving vendors, Form, Eos and the rest, and that raises the effective cost of exactly the technology data centers have been told will firm up renewables.
SAM: Fourth, behind-the-meter in California. Bloom Energy and California Resources signed an MOU to evaluate at least 1 gigawatt of solid oxide fuel cells, capturing the concentrated CO2 in the exhaust and injecting it into California Resources' Class VI storage acreage, for both grid and on-site customers.
ALEX: The context is that Bloom already has an AEP order above 1 gigawatt, a Brookfield partnership that grew to 25 billion dollars in July, and a stock up about 217% over 12 months. The pitch is firm, low-carbon power for California data centers that can't wait for the interconnection queue. Whether it works depends on whether the CPUC and CARB will count fuel cells plus carbon capture as clean firm power, and neither has said so yet.
SAM: Finally, nuclear, with good news and bad news in the same week. The NRC issued X-energy a construction permit for 4 Xe-100 reactors at Dow's Seadrift site in Texas. At the same time, Ontario Power Generation said its Darlington BWRX-300, the lead Western small modular reactor, will miss both its October 2030 target and its 7.7-billion-Canadian-dollar budget, because first-of-a-kind engineering isn't finished.
ALEX: Darlington is the project every SMR plan is benchmarked against. If the lead unit can't hold its schedule, every hyperscaler SMR power purchase agreement with a 2030 start date carries more schedule risk than its press release suggests. Meanwhile China's CNNC poured first concrete on Xuwei Unit 3, its second unit of that design. Taken together, this week's deals price existing gas higher, new storage riskier and new nuclear later.

## SEGMENT FIVE — TAGALOG VOCAB

ALEX: Ang pagdausdos ay ang tuloy-tuloy na pagbaba, parang batang dumadausdos sa padulasan. Hindi ito biglang pagbagsak; dahan-dahan pero walang tigil. Kaya bagay ito sa merkado: ang pagdausdos ng piso, ang pagdausdos ng presyo ng stocks.

ALEX: Halimbawa: Sa kabila ng mga pangako ng gobyerno, hindi pa rin napipigilan ang pagdausdos ng piso. Pormal ang dating nito at madalas sa balitang pang-ekonomiya, pero puwede rin sa araw-araw: ang pagdausdos ng kanyang grado sa eskuwela.
SAM: Ang ikalawang salita: kaalyado.
ALEX: Ang kaalyado ay ang kakampi sa isang pormal na samahan, lalo na sa pulitika o depensa. Mula ito sa salitang alyado, at ang unlaping ka ay nagpapakita ng pagiging magkasama, gaya ng kaibigan, kasama, kababayan. Ang Pilipinas at Amerika ay magkaalyado sa ilalim ng tratado ng depensa.

ALEX: At may kahulugan din itong pampulitika sa loob ng bansa: ang mga kaalyado ng Pangulo sa Kongreso. Halimbawa: Umaasa ang Malacañang na mananatiling tapat ang mga kaalyado nito habang dinidinig ang badyet. Pakinggan natin ang dalawang salitang iyan sa susunod na balita.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
ALEX: Mandarin vocab. Our first item is a collocation: 释放信号, shìfàng xìnhào, to send or release a signal.
SAM: 释放 on its own means to release, as in releasing a prisoner or releasing energy. Paired with 信号 it becomes the standard formal phrase in policy and finance writing for one actor telegraphing intent to another. It's 书面语, the language of central-bank commentary. The pattern is subject plus 释放了 plus an adjective plus 信号: a hawkish signal, a positive signal, a cautious signal.
ALEX: Example. 央行回笼资金，释放了谨慎的信号。(By draining funds, the central bank sent a cautious signal.) The near-synonym is 表明, to indicate or show. 表明 states a fact plainly, while 释放信号 implies intent and an audience: someone is meant to read it.
SAM: Our second item is a structure you'll see across editorials: 以……为代价, yǐ … wéi dàijià, at the cost of something.
ALEX: The thing that's sacrificed goes in the middle. 以增长为代价, at the cost of growth. 以环境为代价, at the cost of the environment. It's formal and slightly critical; it implies the trade-off was a choice and the price was real. Example. 稳定汇率不能以经济增长为代价。(Stabilising the exchange rate cannot come at the cost of economic growth.)
SAM: The near-synonym is 牺牲, to sacrifice, which is a verb you can use on its own and sounds more emotional. 以……为代价 is the analytical framing; it lays the trade-off out as an equation. You'll also hear 付出代价, to pay a price, which works for consequences people suffer. Example. 如果不改革，地方政府迟早要付出代价。(Without reform, local governments will sooner or later pay the price.)

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
ALEX: Op-eds. First, the Washington Post editorial board on Sunday: California voted to make housing more affordable, and more expensive. The argument is about this month's ballot.
SAM: Here's the steelman. Californians passed Proposition 1, an 11.25-billion-dollar housing bond, with 61%, including 7.2 billion for apartments, 1.25 billion for veterans and 1.1 billion in buyer assistance. They passed Proposition 37, up to 25 billion dollars of revenue bonds for second-mortgage down-payment help, available to buyers earning up to 200% of area median income. And they rejected Proposition 45, which would have fast-tracked legal challenges under the environmental review law, CEQA, for housing and infrastructure.
ALEX: So, the Post says, voters added demand subsidies and refused to cut the main brake on supply. Giving more buyers more money to chase the same housing stock raises prices, and the Post calls it a clear example of how not to improve affordability. It's the textbook economics, and on Proposition 37 it's hard to dispute: a down-payment subsidy reaching households at twice the median income is largely capitalised into prices.
SAM: The counter-take is that the Post is treating three measures as one. 7.2 billion dollars for apartments funds actual units, which is supply. And Proposition 45 didn't lose because voters love lawsuits. It bundled housing with other infrastructure, and that gave every opponent of every pipeline and highway a reason to vote no. The lesson for reformers is to sell deregulation separately, not to conclude that voters reject building.
ALEX: Second, Hugo Drochon of Nottingham in Project Syndicate: Can Brexit be reversed? The context is Prime Minister Andy Burnham calling Brexit a mistake and floating going all the way back.
SAM: Drochon's steelman is that Brexit was won by a movement, so only a movement can undo it. Careful diplomacy from Whitehall won't rejoin anything without a popular campaign behind it. And the polling supports him. An October survey found about three quarters of Labour voters want a rejoin pledge in the next manifesto, with majorities among every party's voters except Reform's.
ALEX: The counter is that a movement can't change Brussels's terms. A rejoining Britain would likely face the euro and Schengen commitments and no rebate, and the EU may not want a reluctant member it would have to negotiate with again. And a rejoin campaign gives Reform exactly the cause it needs to mobilise, reopening a culture war Labour had spent years burying. The quieter route of a veterinary deal, youth mobility and perhaps a customs union delivers more of the economic gain at much lower political cost.
SAM: Both pieces make the same mistake, I think: treating the popular vote as the end of the argument. California's voters and Britain's Labour voters can want something without that being enough to get it.
