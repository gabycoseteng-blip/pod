## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

SAM: Deep dive today goes somewhere with no earnings call. On October 27, a team spanning the University of Washington, SLAC, Stanford and the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics announced the first dwarf planet candidate found in data from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. It's called 2026 ED138.
ALEX: To be exact about what's claimed: it is a candidate, it is unconfirmed, and the size is an estimate. The reported range is 600 to 1,500 kilometers across, and that spread is the most interesting number in the story.
SAM: Why is the range so wide? We can't see a disk at that distance, can we?
ALEX: No. At about 46 astronomical units, a bit farther than Pluto's average of roughly 40, it's a point of light. You know how bright it looks, you know how far away it is, so you can compute how much sunlight it reflects, but you can't separate size from reflectivity.
ALEX: A small, shiny object and a large, dark one can look identical. If the surface is icy and bright, 600 kilometers is plenty. If it's dark, like much of the outer solar system's rubble, it has to be bigger to put out the same light. That assumed albedo is the whole 600 to 1,500 spread.
SAM: And infrared follow-up fixes it, because a body's heat glow depends on size in a different way than reflected light does.
ALEX: Exactly. Combine visible and infrared and you solve for both unknowns. For scale, Pluto is about 2,380 kilometers, Eris is about the same, and Ceres is about 940. So at the top of the range this is a Haumea or Makemake class world, and at the bottom it's something smaller than Ceres.
SAM: Which brings in the definition, and the part everyone argues about. What actually makes something a dwarf planet?
ALEX: The International Astronomical Union's 2006 rules. It orbits the Sun, it isn't a moon, and it has enough mass for its own gravity to pull it into a roughly round shape, called hydrostatic equilibrium. A full planet must also have cleared its orbital neighborhood. A dwarf planet hasn't.
SAM: So the criterion is roundness, and nobody has photographed this thing well enough to see whether it's round.
ALEX: Right. In practice, astronomers estimate it. Rock is strong at small sizes and can hold a lumpy shape, ice gives way at a few hundred kilometers, so a body above roughly 400 to 600 kilometers of mostly ice is probably round. That's why the lower end of this range is borderline and the upper end is comfortable.
SAM: There are five officially recognized dwarf planets, Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea and Makemake, and some outlets are calling this a possible sixth. Is that fair?
ALEX: It's fair as a headline and premature as a classification. The IAU doesn't have a fast process, and in practice the recognized list has barely changed since 2008. A lot of astronomers keep unofficial lists of dozens of likely candidates, so what's new here is less the object than how it was found.
SAM: And how was it found? Because that's the piece that matters for the observatory itself.
ALEX: Matthew Holman at the Center for Astrophysics led a search code built with Kevin Napier, and it ran on Rubin data from March to July of 2026. The orbit was then traced backward through archival images to 2016, which is how you get a stable orbit instead of a few weeks of dots.
SAM: So no dedicated telescope time. It's a software story.
ALEX: It's a software story on a data firehose. Rubin's survey is designed to image the whole southern sky repeatedly over 10 years. The telescope doesn't point at targets, it takes everything, so discoveries come from asking new questions of the same pile.
SAM: Which flips how discovery works. The old model was a person with a hunch and a telescope, and the new model is a pipeline and a good linking algorithm.
ALEX: And that matters for the population statistics. If one search found a body this big this far out, the open question is how many more sit in the archive. Distant objects move slowly, and linking detections across years is the hard part, so every improvement in the linking code pulls new bodies out of old data.
SAM: There's a decent chance this isn't a one-off, then.
ALEX: There's a decent chance we're about to get a census instead of a handful of celebrity objects. A census changes the science, because the shape of the distribution, how many bodies at each size and each orbit, tells you how the outer solar system formed and whether something big once shepherded it.
SAM: That's the argument for not getting hung up on the label.
ALEX: Right. Calling it a planet or not is a negotiation, and the data is the real prize. If there turn out to be hundreds of round-ish icy worlds, the 2006 definition looks less like a settled line and more like a freeze-frame of what we could see.
SAM: One caution on the details. Some write-ups cite an orbital period and inclination that we couldn't verify, so we'll stick with what the research team stated: the size range, the distance, and the archival link back to 2016.
ALEX: And the honest status is a candidate with a measurable question. Infrared follow-up tells us whether it's 600 kilometers of bright ice or something Pluto-adjacent, and that single measurement decides which of the two headlines was right.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES

ALEX: Energy, and the lens is deliverable megawatts, because this week's hyperscaler prints all say the same thing from different angles. Amazon's Andy Jassy said even at $220 billion of capex the company will not have enough capacity, and that blocks of hundreds of megawatts up to a gigawatt are being reserved well in advance.
SAM: Yet AWS's sequential dollar add actually slowed, from $4.65 billion to $3.75 billion.
ALEX: Management blamed uneven capacity landing, which is the tell. Demand isn't the constraint. AWS backlog is $578 billion, up $82 billion in a quarter. The revenue line is a proxy for energized capacity, and it moves when buildings get power, not when contracts get signed.
SAM: Alphabet made the same point more bluntly. Its cloud backlog is about $561 billion, roughly 55% recognized within 24 months, and it began using third-party compute capacity at the end of the third quarter.
ALEX: That's the new angle. Google is renting capacity from someone else because it couldn't build fast enough, and its Q3 capex of $51.4 billion was about 65% servers and networking, 35% data centers. The ratio matters, because servers are the part that depreciate on a 5-year clock, whether or not the shell has power.
SAM: Microsoft's version?
ALEX: Fiscal 2027 capex of roughly $170 billion, though sources disagree on whether that's fiscal or calendar, so check the filing. More than $50 billion in the December quarter, and the CFO blamed third-party GPU provider delays for part of the undershoot, pushing some capacity into the first half of 2027.
SAM: So the delivery risk list now includes your neocloud counterparty, not just turbines and transformers.
ALEX: Exactly. Add the chain: you sign a lease, a developer needs interconnection, a utility needs equipment, a chip vendor needs to ship, and a neocloud has to land the racks. Any one late link strands the other four.
SAM: Does that change how you read the capex guides?
ALEX: It changes which line you trust. A capex number tells you what was ordered. The delivered-capacity number, the megawatts energized, is what drives revenue, and none of the three companies disclosed it this quarter. Until that appears, sequential cloud revenue is the best public gauge of what actually got plugged in.
SAM: And the free cash flow hit comes from paying for equipment months before it earns anything.
ALEX: That's the timing mismatch in Amazon's trailing figure. Property and equipment purchases rose $94.8 billion year over year, while the revenue those purchases support arrives only as buildings energize. The gap between the two is the negative free cash flow investors just priced.
SAM: On the supply side, the Department of Energy announced a conditional commitment of up to $5 billion in loans to Constellation for uprates at 8 plants: Braidwood, Byron, Clinton, Dresden, LaSalle, Quad Cities, Calvert Cliffs and Limerick.
ALEX: It adds roughly 1.1 gigawatts, about one large reactor's worth, but per-site megawatts and schedule weren't disclosed, and it's conditional on due diligence. Constellation says it has already committed more than $7 billion for 1,080 megawatts delivering to Google, Amazon and Meta.
SAM: Which means the power is largely pre-sold.
ALEX: So the federal loan lowers the cost of capital on megawatts that already have buyers. It accelerates delivery, but it doesn't create supply beyond the uprate itself, and that's a useful distinction when you hear it described as a new reactor.
SAM: And Texas is filtering the other end of the queue.
ALEX: A Public Utility Commission rule requires $50,000 per megawatt of requested peak demand as financial security, effective October 8, per a law-firm summary, so confirm against the rule text. At that price a 1 gigawatt request posts $50 million.
SAM: Queue size estimates range from about 226 gigawatts to 474, depending on the date and the definition.
ALEX: Which is why a per-megawatt deposit is the right tool. It doesn't shrink the queue by decree, it reprices optionality, and the speculative requests are the first to fall away. Modo Energy tracks around 92 gigawatts of advanced data-center load in ERCOT, and that's the number to hold against the headline queue.
SAM: What's the takeaway for a developer?
ALEX: Capacity is sold out, the utility and the chip vendor set the clock, and credit is moving from the speculative to the committed. The winners are the ones with a signed utility agreement and a delivered shell, not the biggest queue position.
SAM: Does a company like Amazon need to worry about the queue deposit?
ALEX: Not at that scale, because it has balance-sheet-backed utility agreements. The deposit bites the mid-sized developer who stacked ten speculative requests hoping one clears. At $50,000 per megawatt, ten 500 megawatt requests cost $250 million to hold, and that is a very different carrying cost than a free option.
SAM: So expect fewer, firmer requests.
ALEX: And a cleaner signal for utilities about which load will really show up, which is what ratebase planning has been missing.

## SEGMENT FIVE — TAGALOG VOCAB

ALEX: Kaya may "pagtugon sa sakuna," "pagtugon sa krisis," at "pagtugon sa pangangailangan ng mamamayan." Laging may kasamang obligasyon.
SAM: Pansinin ang ugnayan ng dalawa. Kapag umaray ang taumbayan, inaasahan ang pagtugon ng pamahalaan. Isa itong hugis ng usapan sa balita ngayong linggo.
ALEX: Tatandaan natin: umaray, ang pag-daing sa mabigat na gastos, at pagtugon, ang aksyon sa problema. Pakinggan natin pareho sa susunod na bahagi.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

SAM: Two Mandarin words today, both from the currency story. The first is 升值, shēngzhí, to appreciate.
ALEX: 升值 is used for currencies and assets that gain value against something else. 人民币升值了，出口企业的压力就更大。(When the renminbi appreciates, the pressure on exporters grows.)
SAM: And it's different from 上涨, which just means a price goes up. 升值 implies value gained in relative terms, so you wouldn't say a loaf of bread 升值.
ALEX: Right, the opposite is 贬值, depreciate, and common collocations are 人民币升值, 房产升值, and 升值预期, the expectation of appreciation. It's neutral-to-formal, fine in speech and in the news.
SAM: Second word, a pattern: 对……造成冲击, to have a shock impact on something.
ALEX: It's a written, formal collocation. 美元走强对新兴市场造成冲击。(A stronger dollar has a shock impact on emerging markets.)
SAM: How is that different from 影响, which also means affect?
ALEX: 影响 is neutral and covers everything. 冲击 stresses a sudden, disruptive blow, like a wave hitting. You'd use 对出口造成冲击 for a sharp shock to exports, and 影响出口 for a general influence.
SAM: Handy for the news, because markets, the economy and exports are the usual objects after 对. This second one is the abstract collocation to watch for in the China segment.
ALEX: So 升值 for the yuan gaining, and 对……造成冲击 for a blow to something. Both come up in a minute.
