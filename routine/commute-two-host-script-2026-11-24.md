## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: For the deep dive, we're stepping away from markets and into the atmosphere. Adam Tooze's Chartbook newsletter, issue 484, landed in the inbox over the weekend under the headline: Turning point? Is 2026 the moment when China's electrotech revolution drives a downturn in global CO2?
ALEX: And the answer Tooze gives is a qualified yes. Fossil carbon dioxide may finally be turning down, in his words too late and too slowly. Some of it is a shock: the Iran war and the Hormuz crisis have kept oil and gas expensive, and people use less of what costs more. But his argument is that the bigger driver is structural, China's build-out of solar, wind, electric cars, electric trucks and battery swapping. China's emissions have been flat or falling since March 2024.
SAM: Start with the global number.
ALEX: The Global Carbon Project's annual budget estimates fossil CO2 emissions in 2026 at about 38.2 billion tonnes, down 0.4% from last year. By fuel, oil is down 1.9%, gas 0.3%, and coal is still up 0.6%. By country, China is down 0.9%, the US 1.3%, the EU 1.5%, and India is up 4.4%.
SAM: And China specifically?
ALEX: The sharper number comes from Lauri Myllyvirta at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, writing for Carbon Brief. China's third-quarter emissions fell 3.1% year on year, the biggest quarterly drop since the 2022 lockdowns. Coal power was down 3.3% and transport oil down 9%. Tooze has a nice way of putting the scale: 3.1% of China's emissions is roughly the United Kingdom's entire output.
SAM: What's doing the work?
ALEX: Hardware, in volumes that are hard to absorb. Since 2022, China has added roughly 1,000 gigawatts of solar, more than the rest of the world combined, and over 300 gigawatts of wind, about twice America's entire wind fleet. Electric vehicles are around 60% of China's light-vehicle sales. In September, half of all heavy trucks sold were electric, and CATL is running hundreds of highway battery-swap stations for them. Sinopec, the state oil major, expects Chinese oil demand to fall in 2026 for the third year in a row, by nearly 4%.
SAM: So why the question mark in the headline?
ALEX: Because there are 4 good objections, and Tooze takes them seriously. First, shock versus structure. Myllyvirta himself says oil use will rebound once, or if, prices come down. Part of 2026 is the Iran war acting like a carbon tax, and that tax is due to expire. Second, the error bars. The Global Carbon Project's range runs from minus 1.5% to plus 0.6%, so the headline decline could become a rise in the final data. Third, chemicals. China's coal-to-chemicals expansion is the one big industrial sector still growing its emissions, and it could offset gains in power and transport.
SAM: And the fourth?
ALEX: The fourth is the one closest to home for us. Include land use and total CO2 hits a record, about 43.3 billion tonnes, and the driver is fires in drained peatlands and forest degradation in equatorial Asia. So the fossil number may be turning while the total is still climbing, and a large part of the gap is in Southeast Asia.
SAM: Let me push on the politics. A week ago COP31 collapsed without even a cover text. Does this make the diplomacy matter less?
ALEX: It makes it matter differently. The negotiations assume emissions fall when governments agree to make them fall. What Tooze describes is emissions falling because Chinese factories made the alternatives cheaper than the incumbents. Diplomacy failed in Antalya while deployment kept going. The catch is that deployment is concentrated: China cuts, India grows 4.4%, and the swing countries are now the fast-growing emerging economies buying Chinese equipment.
SAM: And what's China doing next?
ALEX: Tooze points to the plan Beijing calls the 6 networks: about 25 trillion yuan, roughly 3.7 trillion dollars, over 5 years, across power, computing, communications, logistics, water and pipelines. The Economist reckons that's about 3% of China's GDP over the period, and Tooze expects it to set the direction of the 15th Five-Year Plan. Power and computing sit in the same plan: the state-led version of America's utility versus data-centre fight.
SAM: Bring it home. Why does this matter to someone building energy projects in Asia?
ALEX: 3 reasons. Cheap Chinese modules and batteries are now a structural fact, not a cyclical glut, so project models built on falling equipment costs are no longer optimistic. The import shock that pushed China off oil is the same shock pushing the peso and Philippine fuel prices around, so the economics of electrification look better from Manila every month that Hormuz stays closed. And the land-use finding puts Southeast Asian peat and forests at the centre of the next round of climate pressure, which will show up in carbon-credit diligence and ESG screens. The remaining 1.5 degree budget is about 150 billion tonnes, less than 4 years at today's rate. So even if this is the turning point, the slope matters more than the peak.
SAM: Turning point, but the slope is what counts. Chartbook 484, if you want the charts.

It's a late-cycle platform purchase, and the contracted share is the number to watch if it ever comes back to market.
SAM: Here's the mirror image, which DealFlow found in a FERC filing.
ALEX: Docket EC27-16-000, filed November 13. 17 Lightshift battery project companies in Massachusetts, Virginia and Vermont are asking FERC for section 203 approval of a sale to Meridiam Infrastructure North America. These projects sell storage services to municipal utilities, Braintree Electric Light Department, the City of Danville, and the Vermont Public Power Supply Authority, under 10 and 20-year agreements. Put it next to Flatiron and you can see the two ends of the market: small, fully contracted, municipal counterparties at the safe end, and 24 gigawatt-hours of mostly merchant optionality at the other.
SAM: Transmission. The Energy Department has offered Grid United a conditional loan of up to 2.8 billion dollars for the Wyoming Intertie.
ALEX: It's about 106 miles of HVDC, 1,800 megawatts, from a converter in Platte County to an existing substation in Carbon County, and it connects SPP in the Eastern Interconnection to WECC in the West. The 30-year loan would cover 80% of construction cost on a project of about 4 billion dollars. The department claims up to 39 billion dollars of ratepayer savings across 17 states.

ALEX: That's the important word. Closing requires full right-of-way, signed capacity contracts and every permit, and the timeline is permits around 2029, construction in 2030 and operation in 2032 or 2033. Those 3 conditions are exactly where interregional lines usually die. So the signal is political, not physical: this administration will lend federal money to stitch the two big grids together, which matters for anyone sizing a data-centre campus near a seam. But it's a 2030s asset.
SAM: In California, Avantus and PG&E have signed for Buttonbush.
ALEX: 800 megawatts of solar and 3,200 megawatt-hours of storage under 2 20-year power purchase agreements, about 3 billion dollars of project, in Kern County. Construction starts in 2027, operation in 2029, and it still needs CPUC approval. No price was disclosed. DealFlow asks who holds the tax-credit risk on a solar plant that starts construction in 2027, after the credit phase-down, and whether that risk sits with the developer or gets priced into the contract. We don't know the answer, but the 4-hour storage attached to 800 megawatts tells you PG&E is buying evening capacity, not daytime energy.
SAM: Quick hits from the CTVC newsletter.
ALEX: Amazon pledged another 7 billion dollars for Indian renewables by 2030, on top of 4 billion already contracted, including 934 megawatts of wind and solar agreements across Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan. ## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Now Mandarin, with 2 words for this morning's China story about Beijing's move on margin rules.
ALEX: The first is 放宽, fàngkuān, to loosen or relax a limit, a rule or a standard. It's written register, the verb of regulators and policy documents, and its opposite is 收紧, to tighten. It collocates with 限制, restrictions, 条件, conditions, 政策, policy, and 标准, standards. Example: 交易所放宽了融资保证金比例。(The exchanges loosened the minimum margin ratio.)
SAM: The near-synonym is 放松, which also means to relax, but it's broader and more everyday. You 放松 your mood or your muscles: 周末好好放松一下。(Relax properly at the weekend.) Policy can be 放松 too, but 放宽 specifically means widening a boundary: the bar is lowered, the limit moved. If a rule has a number in it, it's usually 放宽.
ALEX: The second is 意味着, yìwèizhe, meaning to mean, to signal, or to imply. It's an abstract collocation rather than a concrete word: the subject is a fact, a number or a decision, and the object is its consequence. It sits in the formal, written register of financial news. Example: 融资余额从3万亿元降到2.2万亿元，意味着投资者的信心还没有恢复。(Margin debt falling from 3 trillion yuan to 2.2 trillion means investor confidence hasn't recovered.)
SAM: Contrast it with 说明 and 表示. 说明 means to show or demonstrate: the evidence proves a point. 表示 means to express or state, and it's what a person or an institution does: 央行表示会继续观察。(The central bank said it will keep watching.) 意味着 is about consequence: what a fact implies for what comes next. A rate cut 意味着 cheaper loans; a statement 表示 an intention. Listen for both in the China story next.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
SAM: Op-eds. First, former senator Pat Toomey in the Washington Post, under the headline: Republicans are flirting with a leftist tenet. They need to rethink.
ALEX: Steelman first. Toomey argues that after the midterm defeat, the populist wing of his party has adopted the left's core premise, scepticism of free markets, along with its policies: tariffs that by design raise what families pay, and government ownership, like Washington's 10% stake in Intel. SAM: Second, an Economist leader: America must get serious about Chinese chip spying.
ALEX: The argument: the Nvidia affair, and here the links to a suspected Chinese agent remain allegations, has exposed an underworld of AI-chip smuggling and espionage that Washington handles in a disorganised way. Work with allies on industrial espionage. And it concedes the US can't stop China's chipmaking progress outright.
SAM: Counter-take?
ALEX: Monday answered part of it. Nvidia's board and the market treated the affair as key-man risk to be solved by bringing Huang back, not as a security breach to be solved by controls, and the White House pushed for that outcome. So the political will for serious counter-espionage is weaker than the leader assumes. And a kill-switch regime gives Beijing every incentive to accelerate exactly the self-sufficiency the Economist admits it can't stop. Leverage you announce is leverage the other side builds around.
