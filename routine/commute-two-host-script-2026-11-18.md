## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: For the deep dive we're leaving markets for the Indian Ocean. This comes from The Economist, which the listener gets in her inbox: a piece headlined Inside America's first new submarine base abroad for 53 years. The thesis in the blurb is that AUKUS is starting to make waves on Australia's west coast. The Economist visited HMAS Stirling on Garden Island, off Perth, which is getting ready to host American nuclear-powered attack submarines from 2027, a port, as they put it, that will project American power deep into Asia.
ALEX: The details behind it are public. The arrangement is called Submarine Rotational Force-West. From 2027 up to 4 US Virginia-class boats and 1 British Astute-class boat will rotate through Stirling. In June the US Navy quietly set up 2 commands there, Naval Support Activity Stirling and Submarine Squadron 3. The first named boat is USS Colorado, a Block III Virginia, which arrived on October 27 for about a month of maintenance, with Australian technicians working alongside staff from Pearl Harbor Naval Shipyard. It goes home and comes back in late 2027 to start the rotations properly.
SAM: The interesting fight is over one word. The Economist calls it a base, and Canberra very much does not.
ALEX: Australia has a long-standing policy against foreign bases, so officially the boats are rotating, not based. In practice there are 2 standing US commands, a dedicated maintenance workforce, and Australia is spending up to 8 billion Australian dollars over the next decade to expand the facility, with about 3,000 direct jobs and a 281 million dollar wharf due to finish in 2027. Australia's regulator also approved storage of low-level radioactive waste at Stirling back in July 2024. If the infrastructure is permanent, the commands are permanent and the maintenance is local, then rotational starts to look like a legal formula rather than an operational reality. The last time the US opened a submarine base abroad was La Maddalena in Sardinia in the early 1970s, which is where the 53-year count comes from, and that one closed in 2008.
SAM: Why does Washington want it? The map. Perth is a long way from the South China Sea, but it's far closer than Guam is to the Indian Ocean approaches, the Lombok and Sunda straits, and it's well outside the dense Chinese missile envelope that covers the first island chain.
ALEX: That's the strategic logic: a rear area that's secure, deep water close to the boats' patrol areas, and an ally that pays. Which brings us to the money, because that's the most contested part. The whole AUKUS program is priced at around 368 billion Australian dollars. Australia has already paid 3 billion US dollars into the American submarine industrial base, all of it non-refundable, with the latest 1.5 billion Australian dollar instalment last December. The deal is that Australia buys 3 to 5 Virginia-class boats in the 2030s before its own AUKUS-class boats arrive.
SAM: And the US yards can't build them fast enough.
ALEX: Not close. Virginia-class output is running at about 1.1 to 1.15 boats a year. The target that covers both US needs and Australia's sales is 2.33 a year, with 2 as the interim goal. The US Navy's chief, Admiral Daryl Caudle, has said a steady 2 a year is unlikely before the 2030s. And US law requires the president to certify that selling boats to Australia won't erode the US fleet. So there's a real scenario where Australia funds a production line, hosts the American boats, and then can't buy any of its own on time. The Pentagon's AUKUS review, run by Elbridge Colby under an America First lens, reportedly finished around January, and its findings still haven't been published.
SAM: So what are the critics actually saying?
ALEX: There are 3 lines of attack. The sovereignty critique, from a strand of the defence commentariat and a crowdfunded public inquiry, says Stirling ties Australia into US war plans, because in a Taiwan contingency you can't plausibly claim a port hosting American attack boats is neutral. Deputy Prime Minister Richard Marles answers that AUKUS builds sovereign capability, not dependence. The target critique, from local activists, says Rockingham and Perth become nuclear-era targets. The Lowy Institute calls that overheated, since any serious war would put Australian infrastructure at risk anyway. And there's a consent critique: groups including Stop AUKUS WA argue the Traditional Owners of Garden Island never gave free, prior and informed consent, and raise worries about Cockburn Sound and the waste.
SAM: Here's my take on why it matters this week in particular. It lands while Trump is courting Xi and Putin at the same table in Shenzhen.
ALEX: That's the second-order read. Allies are being asked to make 30-year commitments, concrete wharves and nuclear waste licences, on the strength of a relationship with a Washington that's openly transactional. Stirling is Australia's bet that hosting American hardware is the best insurance policy available. The Economist's framing, that this is America projecting power deep into Asia, is true. But the other side is just as true: Australia is paying for the privilege, in money and in risk, before it has a signed delivery date for a single boat. Hosting the boats is proof of trust. What tests the deal is whether the boats Australia paid for actually arrive.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: To energy, and Monday's filing deadline turned into the most important day of the year for large-load interconnection. PJM filed 2 packages at FERC answering the commission's June show-cause orders, and NYISO, MISO, CAISO and ISO-New England all filed the same day.
ALEX: Start with PJM's Fast Energization package, all 1,067 pages of it. It studies generation, transmission upgrades and large loads together in a single process instead of in sequence. PJM projects that takes the study clock from 24 months to 12, though that's PJM's projection, not a measured result. It creates a new transmission service for large customers and shows states up front what the upgrades will cost. PJM wants a 60-day comment period and an order within 120 days, which points to a ruling around mid-March. Remember that in October FERC kept PJM's 50-megawatt large-load definition and the registry, but rejected the curtail-first service. This is PJM's second attempt, and it's built around the cost transparency FERC asked for.
SAM: The second filing is the one with real engineering consequences for campuses.
ALEX: It's a ride-through requirement for what PJM calls Large Computational Loads: 50 megawatts or more in aggregate, with at least 25 megawatts behind power-electronic conversion. Those sites must ride through grid disturbances instead of tripping over to onsite generation. The trigger was July 22, when roughly 3.9 gigawatts of data-center load moved to backup generation at once during a disturbance, the largest load transfer in PJM's history. PJM wants an order by January 15 and the rule live January 16, and existing sites get 1 year to comply.
SAM: Which means retrofit capex for every operating campus above 50 megawatts in PJM.
ALEX: Exactly. Ride-through is now an interconnection condition, so UPS topology, protection settings and inverter firmware become something the grid operator regulates, not just a design choice. The flip-to-generators-at-the-first-sag strategy that a lot of sites rely on for uptime becomes non-compliant. And ISO-New England went furthest on transparency: it would publish every large load's network upgrades and cost estimates on its website. SPP files on Friday. Data-center groups are already arguing the 12-month clock will slip, and the timing could collide with PJM's December capacity auction, which the market monitor wants postponed after attributing about 29.4 billion dollars of capacity revenue to data-center load growth.
SAM: Here's a number for the gap between announced and real. Latitude Media launched a tracker on Monday that counts about 120 gigawatts of signed data-center power contracts against 537 gigawatts of load reported by utilities.
ALEX: So about 22% coverage, which means roughly 78% of the load in utility forecasts has no signed contract behind it. That's the exposure if collateral rules and ride-through filters strip out speculative requests, and it's a real risk for any integrated resource plan sized on the 537. On the supply side, fewer than a third of contracted loads plan to be served carbon-free, and 59% of signed load sits in states with no carbon-free requirement. Dominion alone is seeking up to 7.3 gigawatts of dispatchable generation.
SAM: Meanwhile CTVC, in the listener's inbox on Monday, broke down the cost clause in the federal permitting bill, BAAJA.
ALEX: The new detail is cost allocation. Large loads would pay their share of the existing grid plus the full cost of the new transmission upgrades they trigger. Data centers would report energy use to the EIA, FERC would get backstop siting authority for interstate lines of 230 kilovolts and up without needing a DOE corridor designation, and grid-enhancing technologies like reconductoring and dynamic line ratings become mandatory wherever benefits exceed costs. Rhodium puts household savings at 50 to 78 dollars a year. CTVC quotes Tim Fox at Crux: the cost of capital now includes the cost of consent. Put that next to Monday's filings and the direction is the same at the federal and the RTO level: you pay for the wires you cause. CTVC calls behind-the-meter gas setups shakier under that regime, because their argument was partly that they avoided those costs.
SAM: On deals, DealFlow led this week with a question: why is Arevon selling 92 small operating projects to Altus Power instead of running them itself?
ALEX: It's about 200 megawatts of solar plus 10 megawatt-hours of storage across 13 states, mostly New York, New Jersey, Massachusetts and California, partly funded off Altus's credit facility with Blackstone. Altus gets to about 1.7 gigawatts operating. The answer to DealFlow's question is focus and fund life: distributed assets don't scale for a developer chasing utility-scale and data-center-adjacent projects, and a consolidator wins on maintenance density. A guest piece in the same issue notes that 10 of the 13 clean-energy platform deals since June had a private-equity or infrastructure fund on at least one side, driven by 2020 to 2022 vintage funds in harvest mode, not data-center pull. And 1 last data point: Fermi America disclosed an agreement with an unnamed investment-grade hyperscaler for an initial 600 megawatts at Project Matador outside Amarillo. That's the first investment-grade anchor for a large behind-the-meter campus, and the first real test of whether that model survives the pay-your-way rules coming in.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Now Mandarin, with 2 items tied to this morning's China story about JD.com.
ALEX: The first is 以…为主, yǐ…wéi zhǔ, meaning to be mainly X, or to rely chiefly on X. It's a written, formal frame, standard in earnings commentary and policy reports. The object goes between 以 and 为主. The spoken equivalent is 主要靠, which means the same thing but sounds conversational. Example: 京东的利润增长以降本为主。(JD's profit growth comes mainly from cutting costs.)
SAM: The near-synonym worth contrasting is 主要是. 主要是 just labels the main item, while 以…为主 frames a whole structure and implies the other parts are secondary. You'll see it constantly in descriptions of economies: 中国的增长曾经以投资为主，现在要以消费为主。(China's growth used to be driven mainly by investment; now it's supposed to be driven mainly by consumption.)
ALEX: The second is 勉强, miǎnqiǎng, meaning barely, or reluctantly. Used as an adverb it means you just about managed something, with a sense of strain. Compare 刚好, which is neutral and means just right. If revenue 刚好 hit the target, that's fine; if it 勉强 hit the target, it struggled. Example: 这个季度，营收勉强增长了3.5%。(This quarter, revenue barely grew 3.5%.)
SAM: And it has a second, more personal meaning: to force someone, or to do something unwillingly. 你不想去就别勉强自己。(If you don't want to go, don't force yourself.) It works in both spoken and written Chinese, and the finance-desk usage is the adverb.
ALEX: Listen for both in the China story next.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
SAM: Op-eds. The first is Chrystia Freeland in a New York Times guest essay headlined Welcome Back, America! It's Your Turn to Be Sorry. Canada's former deputy prime minister opens with an older Boston couple who apologised to her at an airport after the midterms.
ALEX: The steelman runs like this. The midterms show American democracy still works: voters checked the administration through the system, which is what allies need to see. So Canadians who want a permanent break are wrong. Freeland wants Democrats and the Republicans willing to break with Trump to rebuild trust in 3 areas: tariffs and trade, Ukraine and security, and AI. SAM: The counter-take?
ALEX: A midterm win gives Congress power over the budget and investigations. It doesn't give it power over tariffs, which the executive still sets, or over foreign policy. And the swing can reverse in 2028. Allies who keep hedging, Canada diversifying its trade or Europe building its own defence, are being rational, not resentful. Freeland's own last line makes the point: verification is the hedge. The apology at the airport is nice, but it isn't a treaty.
SAM: The second is Kurt Campbell, Biden's deputy secretary of state, also in the Times on Tuesday, on the Shenzhen summit with Xi and Putin. His line is that Trump is drawn to strongmen like a deer drawn toward the intriguing smell of the hunters' blind.
ALEX: The steelman: look at the record. The Alaska summit with Putin in 2025 produced nothing on Ukraine. The Xi meetings produced little on trade and nothing on AI safeguards. A US president meeting both leaders together is unprecedented, and it hands them the stage and the photographs. Campbell's sharpest charge is that the administration completely misreads the Russia-China relationship, treating it as something Washington can split when it's actually a partnership both sides value too much to trade away.
SAM: And the counter?
ALEX: Talking isn't conceding. Nixon's opening to China required sitting in a room with people the US distrusted, and an American official describes Shenzhen as an entry point on nuclear arms control, not a grand bargain. There's also an awkward point for Campbell: the Biden team's strategy of isolating Russia arguably pushed Moscow and Beijing closer together. A fair verdict is that he's right about the theatre and the record of past summits, and weaker on what the alternative is. Judge Shenzhen on what gets signed and verified, not on the photos.
