## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Our deep dive today comes from this week's Economist Today email, which flagged a piece under the headline "Sanctions, schmanctions: Ireland is helping supply Russia's war machine." We only have the teaser, so we've rebuilt the substance from open reporting by the Irish Times, OCCRP, RTE, Semafor and others. It's a story about a single factory.
ALEX: The factory is Aughinish Alumina, on the Shannon estuary in County Limerick. It is the largest alumina refinery in Europe, and it is owned by Rusal, the Russian aluminium giant tied to the oligarch Oleg Deripaska. Alumina is the intermediate product: bauxite goes in, alumina comes out, and alumina gets smelted into aluminium metal.
SAM: And here's the loophole. The EU has banned exporting aluminium metal to Russia. It has never banned exporting alumina, the intermediate. So Aughinish, a sanctioned oligarch's asset sitting inside the European Union, can legally ship its product to Rusal's smelters in Siberia.
ALEX: The numbers are striking. Exports from Aughinish to Russia in 2025 were 775,985 tonnes, roughly 64,665 tonnes a month. The Irish Times reported on September 1st that almost 49% of its exports are going to Russia this year, up from 43%. Semafor puts it at 68% of 2025 output. The definitions differ, denominator versus exports, but every version points the same direction.
SAM: The route is almost comic in its ordinariness. Bauxite arrives from Brazil and Guinea, is refined in Limerick, and the alumina goes by ship to Novy Port near Saint Petersburg, then about 5,000 kilometers by rail to the smelters in Siberia.
ALEX: And the downstream matters. Russian smelters sold more than $650 million of aluminium to a trader called ASK, which supplies over 40 Russian arms makers that are themselves under EU sanctions. That's the argument in the Economist's framing: nobody is smuggling anything. The supply chain is legal at every link, and the metal still ends up in weapons.
SAM: So why hasn't it been fixed? The Commission has left alumina out of previous sanctions packages, partly because Aughinish supplies EU industry, and partly because of Ireland.
ALEX: The Irish government has lobbied to keep the plant off sanctions lists. The company says alumina sales are legal, that jobs are at stake, and that only a state buyout would avoid mass layoffs. It has even warned that sanctions would hit the local power grid. The job counts conflict, though: about 450 direct in some accounts, about 1,000 direct plus 1,000 supplier jobs in others. In a country where the plant is a big employer in Limerick, that number is political.
SAM: But the pressure is mounting. The European Parliament passed a non-binding resolution on July 8th urging a ban on alumina exports to Russia. Sweden's tax authority said on July 1st that Rusal's European operations should fall under EU sanctions, finding that Deripaska still controls the company. And Fianna Fáil MEPs, from the governing party in Dublin, abstained on the parliamentary vote.
ALEX: There's a smaller tell too. Aughinish was suspended from Ireland's ex-ante electricity market on July 24th of last year, and 13 ships serving the plant have been detained over five years. This isn't a plant nobody has noticed.
SAM: The decision point is the EU's 21st sanctions package, being negotiated now with the aim of adoption before winter. Foreign Minister Helen McEntee has said Ireland would work with the Commission if it proposes a ban.
ALEX: Which is diplomatic language for we won't veto it but don't ask us to volunteer. And here's the second-order read for an audience that works in industrial supply chains. Sanctions leak not through smuggling but through the intermediate good the drafters forgot. Alumina is one; you can name the next: certain polymers, machine-tool components, chip-adjacent chemicals. Every package is a negotiation between a Commission that wants to close the gap and a member state that has one specific factory it wants to protect.
SAM: And the counter-argument deserves its due. If Europe bans alumina and the plant closes, Russia can buy alumina from Jamaica, Australia or Brazil within months. Rusal is not short of suppliers. So the sanction may cost Europe jobs and Russia only a freight premium.
ALEX: That's the strongest objection, and it's why the real test is a buyout or a forced sale to a non-Russian owner rather than an export ban. Package 21 will tell us whether Brussels is willing to say which it wants.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
ALEX: Start with the Department of Energy's emergency coal orders, because a federal appeals court just told DOE it's using the statute wrong. On September 11th the D.C. Circuit ruled that DOE's May 2025 order keeping the J.H. Campbell coal plant in Michigan open was unlawful. The court said Section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act is for genuine short-term emergencies, not a substitute for state resource planning.
SAM: And DOE has responded by continuing to issue orders. Two Indiana plants, Schahfer and Culley, are ordered to run September 20th through December 18th. Craig Unit 1 in Colorado got its fourth 90-day extension on September 25th, running through December 25th, which is a full year past its planned retirement.
ALEX: The rolling 90-day structure looks designed to outrun litigation: every order expires before a court can rule on it, then gets renewed. Consumers Energy has booked $295 million in costs at Campbell from May 2025 through June 2026, against a retirement plan that would have saved about $600 million. Craig is estimated at $85 million to $150 million a year, and nobody has decided who pays. That cost-allocation fight is where this lands next, at FERC and in state rate cases.
SAM: Second item, a supply-chain one. Executive Order 14420, issued under the emergency economic powers statute, covers foreign-made transformers, inverters, battery storage systems, reactors and controls in the bulk power system. It applies to transactions started after August 26th, but nothing is actually barred until DOE finds a specific risk, and DOE's implementing rules aren't due until December 24th.
ALEX: So it's a sword hanging over every procurement decision. Utility Dive reported Tuesday that Energy Vault is assessing its pipeline project by project: 150 megawatts and 300 megawatt-hours near Houston, 175 megawatts and 350 megawatt-hours in northeast Texas, and a powered-land project with Crusoe in west Texas. Nobody can price the risk, so nobody can close on financing without a contingency clause.
SAM: And that lands on top of the transformer bottleneck you already know. The lead time problem plus an unresolved sourcing rule is a recipe for slipped in-service dates.
ALEX: Which brings us to New York. NYSERDA announced its first bulk storage awards on September 23rd: 950 megawatts of lithium-ion across 8 projects at 4 to 8 hour durations, chosen from 46 bids totaling about 6 gigawatts. Plus 719 megawatts of solar, wind and hydro. In total about 1.7 gigawatts and more than $3.7 billion of private investment, all due in service by the end of 2030.
SAM: Roughly 6 gigawatts bid for 950 awarded is a 6-to-1 oversubscription, which says developers are still hungry for storage offtake. But no strike prices were published, and those 2030 in-service dates are exposed to the equipment order we just discussed.
ALEX: There's also a market-design fight in the Southwest Power Pool. The SPP market monitor has panned the stop-gap resource adequacy plan for SPP's western balancing area, in Colorado and Wyoming, now before FERC. The monitor says it blocks economic inter-regional trade, is gameable, and unfairly penalizes participants, and WAPA and Basin Electric have objected too. Thin on numbers, but it matters because the West's markets expansion is the template for where capacity obligations go.
SAM: And the number of the day comes from SemiAnalysis, whose China Datacenter Model published September 25th maps over 1,000 buildings across more than 60 operators. China has delivered over 24 gigawatts of data-center capacity, with about 20 gigawatts more in the pipeline, compared with about 14 gigawatts for all of Europe, the Middle East and Africa.
ALEX: The operating detail is the story. ByteDance alone rents about a fifth of national capacity, and Chinese developers deliver a 100 megawatt facility in about 12 months, while a comparable US project fights interconnection queues for years. Chinese hyperscalers' overseas leasing is set to double to about 4 gigawatts by 2029. So the contrast for a US developer is not chips, it's cycle time: China's constraint is compute supply under export controls, America's is megawatts and permits.
SAM: Put the three US items together and you get a coherent picture: a federal government using emergency powers to keep old capacity online, threatening to restrict the new equipment that would replace it, while the states procure storage that depends on that equipment.
ALEX: Right, and for a developer the practical response is contingency structuring. Domestic-content clauses, dual-sourced transformers, and change-in-law protections in the PPAs. The buyers most exposed are the ones with 2029 and 2030 in-service commitments, because a December rule leaves under 4 years of runway to re-source. Anyone modeling storage returns in New York should add a sourcing-delay scenario now, not after DOE publishes.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
ALEX: Two words today, both from the China story coming up. The first is 补贴, bǔtiē, meaning subsidy.
SAM: It's a written-register, policy-language word, the kind you see in State Council documents and financial headlines. A close neighbor is 补助, bǔzhù, but that leans toward welfare-style aid to individuals, while 补贴 is a policy tool that covers a cost gap, like an interest or price difference.
ALEX: The collocation to know is 房贷利息补贴, mortgage interest subsidy. For example: 政府给首次购房者提供房贷利息补贴。(The government provides a mortgage interest subsidy to first-time home buyers.)
SAM: The second word is a connective, which is your weak spot, so pay attention. 何况, hékuàng, means let alone or what's more. It builds an escalating argument: something is already true for the easy case, so it's certainly true for the harder one.
ALEX: The classic pattern is 连…都…，何况…, which means even X does this, let alone Y. For example: 连专业人士都看不懂，何况普通老百姓呢？(Even professionals can't make sense of it, let alone ordinary people.)
SAM: Register-wise, 何况 works in both speech and writing. Its near-synonym 甚至, meaning even, which you've already met, just adds emphasis. 何况 carries a logical push: it says the bigger case follows automatically. Listen for both words in the China segment.

## SEGMENT EIGHT — CHINA
ALEX: 尽管经济仍然疲软，北京终于出台了新的楼市补贴政策。(Although the economy is still weak, Beijing has finally rolled out a new property subsidy policy.)
SAM: That resolves yesterday's Chinese cliffhanger, a package promised but not yet written. It was written on September 29th. From October 1st, first-home buyers get a 1 percentage point mortgage interest subsidy for up to 5 years, on homes up to 120 square meters and 1.5 million yuan.
ALEX: 连专家都觉得力度有限，何况市场呢？(Even experts think the scale is limited, let alone the market.)
SAM: The design is the story. It's a demand-side subsidy for buyers rather than a developer bailout, which is the opposite of the 2021 to 2024 instinct. The People's Bank of China also cut the rate on its pledged supplementary lending, PSL, to 1.5% from 1.75%, and widened PSL's remit beyond housing to power grids, computing and pipelines.
ALEX: That widening is the tell. PSL is now a general-purpose policy-bank funding channel, and the target list, grids and computing and pipelines, reads like an industrial strategy, not a property rescue. Property stocks fell anyway, because a 1 point subsidy on a 1.5 million yuan cap doesn't clear a market that has had roughly 10 straight quarters of deflation in home prices.
SAM: There was some good news in the data. Official PMIs for September recovered: manufacturing 50.1, up from 49.8, and non-manufacturing 50.2, up from 49.0. Back above the line dividing expansion from contraction.
ALEX: Through the two-speed lens: production and infrastructure are bouncing, households aren't. Until buyers believe prices have stopped falling, a subsidy just lowers the cost of catching a falling knife. That's why this matters for the debt-deflation story: policy is now writing checks to the household side, but at a scale that treats the symptom.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
ALEX: Two columns. First, Senator Josh Hawley in the Washington Post on September 29th, arguing that harms caused by artificial intelligence should carry legal liability. He backs the AI LEAD Act, which he's written with Democrat Dick Durbin, and it treats AI systems as products, with developers liable for defects and required to pre-test before deployment.
SAM: The steelman is strong. Developers shouldn't be able to hide behind the claim that an autonomous agent did it, and product liability has long been the mechanism society uses to price risk into design. If a car's brakes fail, the manufacturer pays, and that incentive is why cars got safer.
ALEX: The counter is that strict liability is a moat. A frontier lab with legal staff and insurance can absorb it, but open-source developers and small labs can't, so the practical effect is consolidation into the incumbents Hawley says he wants to check. And AI outputs depend on how the user prompts, so it's not clear what the defect even is.
SAM: The second is lighter. Katie Roiphe, also in the Post, argues that journaling may actually harm, by freezing rumination into ritual instead of releasing it.
ALEX: The counter is Pennebaker's expressive writing research, decades of studies where people who wrote about stressful events showed health benefits. The synthesis is probably that structured, time-limited writing helps and open-ended brooding does not, which is a narrower claim than Roiphe's headline.
SAM: And back to Hawley, the political read is that AI liability is one of the few issues where a populist Republican and a liberal Democrat like Durbin agree, which makes it more likely to move than the usual tech regulation fights.
ALEX: But note who is missing: the industry lobby's preferred alternative, a federal preemption of state AI laws, cuts the other way. So the real fight in Congress is between liability plus state authority on one side and preemption on the other, and the columns like this one are positioning for it.
