## SEGMENT — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Today's Deep Dive comes from a feature in The Generalist newsletter — Part Two of a series called "Saplings," this installment titled "Unstable Ground." It's not a markets story at all, and it's a genuinely good one.
ALEX: The series studies the early lives of legendary founders, looking for patterns in what shaped them before they built anything. Part Two is specifically about the home — the kind of instability that recurs again and again in these childhoods.
SAM: The opening story is Amadeo Giannini, who founded what became Bank of America. In 1876, when he was six years old, he watched a farmhand shoot his father dead in a dispute over a dollar of unpaid wages. That night, his mother told him: "This is the last time I take you on my knees. From today, you are the man of the house."
ALEX: That pattern — early loss, followed by an abrupt inheritance of adult responsibility — shows up across an enormous list of names. Stan Shih of Acer, George Eastman of Kodak, Fred Smith of FedEx, Jerry Yang of Yahoo, Carlos Slim, Aristotle Onassis, Harland Sanders of KFC, James Dyson, Jim Casey of UPS — all lost a parent in childhood or adolescence.
SAM: Li Ka-Shing's version is almost identical to Giannini's. His father was dying of tuberculosis when Li was fifteen, and told him "a man must have ambitions." Li promised him, in tears, "I will learn to do business and make lots of money" — and within three years had gone from sweeping factory floors to the company's star salesman.
ALEX: Jorge Paulo Lemann's father was struck and killed by a streetcar when Lemann was fourteen — the same immediate shift into being "the man of the house." The piece's argument is that parental death doesn't just create grief, it compresses childhood into a kind of forced adulthood, and pushes the bereaved child to rely entirely on themselves earlier than anyone should have to.
SAM: Not every case in the piece is death, either. Larry Ellison found out he was adopted at around twelve, over dinner, with zero elaboration — his words later were something like, "It was like, 'Tonight we're having meatloaf, and by the way, you're adopted.'" No trauma of loss, but the same effect — the story he'd been standing on turned out not to be true.
ALEX: And then there's Ted Turner, whose father agreed to sell part of the family business and then killed himself the very next day, when Turner was twenty-four. Turner spent the rest of his life visibly obsessed with mortality — friends describe him talking about his own potential suicide constantly, almost flippantly, for years afterward.
SAM: The author's framing, and it's a fair one, is that this isn't a checklist for producing a founder — it's not statistical, it doesn't claim causation. It's an accounting of a pattern he finds genuinely meaningful: early loss, a disappointing father paired with a more dominant mother, love that had to be earned rather than given freely, status that moved rather than stayed fixed, and family expectations placed on a child's shoulders before they were old enough to choose them.
ALEX: What I find most useful about it isn't the individual anecdotes, dramatic as they are — it's the throughline that instability absorbed very young seems to produce a particular relationship to risk later. If you've already learned as a child that your circumstances can change completely overnight, taking a big swing as an adult doesn't feel as reckless as it would to someone who never had that lesson forced on them.
SAM: There's a less romantic reading too, worth holding alongside the inspiring one — survivorship bias is doing real work in a piece like this. Nobody's writing a "Saplings" essay about the thousand kids who lost a parent young and didn't found anything. We only hear about the instability when it's attached to an eventual bank or an airline.
ALEX: Fair, and the author actually flags that himself — this isn't a formula, it's a study of the people who happened to become extraordinary, read backward. Still worth the read for what it says about risk tolerance and self-reliance, even if you're skeptical of drawing a straight line from childhood loss to entrepreneurial success.
SAM: It's a good palate-cleanser before we head back into markets — a reminder that behind every one of these company names is an actual person shaped by something that happened decades before the first product ever launched.

## SEGMENT TWO — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
ALEX: Staying with something structural — this time the AI buildout itself. In Colorado, Xcel's regulated utility PSCo is asking the state Supreme Court to unlock eminent domain powers over a single gen-tie line, and the numbers behind it are wild.
SAM: Two wind farms — Singing Grass at 603 megawatts and Cheyenne Ridge Two at 450 — are sitting essentially finished, about 1.05 gigawatts and something like 1.8 billion dollars of capital, stranded over a dispute involving roughly 550 feet of easement.
ALEX: A district court ruled back in June that Colorado law doesn't let a regulated utility condemn land for a generation-tie line the way it can for public transmission — the reasoning being a gen-tie serves one private project, not the grid broadly. So PSCo can't force the last landowner to sell.
SAM: And PSCo didn't help its own case — it reportedly offered under 20,000 dollars for that 550-foot segment, when it paid 585,000 dollars for 90 acres in the same general area back in 2022, and NextEra settled with that very same landowner at market rate for its nearby Dusty Rose project.
ALEX: That's the real story underneath the legal one — a utility trying to lowball the one landowner it actually needs, and now two nearly-finished wind farms are stuck because of it. Colorado's legislature already moved back in March to curb Xcel's eminent domain power generally, so the political wind is against them too.
SAM: The practitioner read here is that this becomes real precedent — if the Supreme Court agrees a gen-tie doesn't count as public use, every last-mile landowner in Colorado, and in states with similar statutes, just gained enormous leverage over interconnection routing. That's a chokepoint that has nothing to do with queue reform or turbine supply.
ALEX: Speaking of turbine supply — Mitsubishi Power's gas turbine backlog just hit 35 gigawatts, up 52% year-on-year, and orders booked this quarter are already slotting into 2028 through 2030 delivery.
SAM: Put that next to what we already know — Siemens Energy sitting around 70 gigawatts, GE Vernova at 116. All three major OEMs are now effectively fully booked into the next decade. This isn't one company being cautious, it's the entire global turbine supply chain maxed out at once.
ALEX: There's a second bottleneck getting far less attention than turbines, though, and it might matter more. A startup called Heron Power, founded by an ex-Tesla battery executive, is building a 100-million-dollar factory outside San Jose to mass-produce a chip-based, software-controlled alternative to the standard steel-and-oil grid transformer.
SAM: Standard transformers are running lead times of up to four years right now — arguably the single biggest hardware chokepoint behind interconnection delays, longer even than the turbine wait. Heron's targeting mass production of its solid-state unit by late 2027, more than 600 jobs, up to 10,000 units a year at full run rate.
ALEX: Whether silicon actually beats copper and steel at this scale remains to be proven — but it's a genuinely different kind of bet than anything else we've covered in this space, substituting semiconductors for heavy industrial manufacturing rather than just trying to build more of the same thing faster.
SAM: And Texas just handed us the clearest evidence yet that all of this matters. ERCOT hit a new all-time peak demand of 91.1 gigawatts on July 22nd, six percent above the old record — but Ascend Analytics is now warning that more than 80% of the new large loads chasing power in Texas won't actually have matching generation online by 2030.
ALEX: The interconnection queue has grown by over 200 gigawatts since 2024, and Ascend puts the realistic completion rate at only about 55%. So even setting aside the politics of the queue-cull fight we've covered before, there's a hard physical cap here — roughly half the projects that get through the process still won't have power to run on.
SAM: Three different bottlenecks, three different fixes being tried — a legal fight over 550 feet of land, a chip company betting against a four-year transformer wait, and a state grid operator's own forecaster admitting the math doesn't work at current build rates. None of them are about capital anymore. Capital's the one input in this cycle that's never actually been the constraint.

## SEGMENT FOUR — VOCAB OF THE DAY
ALEX: 好，来看看今天的两个中文词。第一个跟今天的能源新闻有关——"不容忽视"。
SAM: "不容忽视"的意思是"cannot be tolerated to be ignored"，也就是不能被忽视。今天Ascend Analytics的报告说，到2030年，得州超过八成的大型用电项目可能拿不到匹配的发电量——这个风险不容忽视。
ALEX: 翻译过来就是：this risk cannot be ignored — by 2030, more than 80% of Texas's large power projects may not have matching generation available。
SAM: "不容忽视"是比较正式的书面语，常用在政策报告、分析师笔记里，后面常搭配"问题"、"风险"、"趋势"。跟"值得关注"比，"不容忽视"语气强得多——"值得关注"只是提醒你注意，"不容忽视"是警告你，不管它后果会很严重。
ALEX: 第二个词跟中国今天的经济数据有关——"由此可见"。
SAM: "由此可见"的意思是"from this it can be seen that"，专门用来从已经摆出的事实里推出结论。今天中国七月的工业增加值和零售数据同时不及预期，可以说：由此可见，出口和消费两方面同时放缓，问题不只是外部需求。
ALEX: 翻译：from this it can be seen that both exports and consumption are slowing at the same time — this isn't just an external-demand problem。
SAM: "由此可见"是很正式的过渡词，一般放在句子开头，专门总结前面的证据。比简单的"可见"更强调推理过程；平时聊天更常直接说"看来"或者"看样子"，没有这么严谨的论证语气。
ALEX: 好，从中文换到菲律宾语。Ngayon, tara na sa Tagalog. Una, "katiwalian."
SAM: "Katiwalian" ay nangangahulugang corruption o malfeasance — hindi lang basta pangungurakot, kundi ang pang-aabuso ng tiwalang ibinigay sa isang opisyal. Halimbawa: Idinetalye ni dating DPWH Sekretaryo Bonoan ang umano'y katiwalian sa 24-bilyong-pisong "leadership fund."
ALEX: Ibig sabihin: former DPWH Secretary Bonoan detailed the alleged corruption in the 24-billion-peso "leadership fund." Iyan mismo ang balitang sumabog ngayong linggo.
SAM: "Katiwalian" ay mas malakas at mas pormal kaysa sa kolokyal na "korap" — ang "korap" parang tsismis o kaswal na akusasyon, habang ang "katiwalian" ang salitang ginagamit sa balita, sa investigative reports, at sa mismong kaso sa Sandiganbayan.
ALEX: Pangalawa, "pag-aalinlangan."
SAM: "Pag-aalinlangan" ay nangangahulugang hesitation o uncertainty — ang pagdududa kung dapat kumilos o hindi. Halimbawa: Dahil sa mahinang GDP, nasa pag-aalinlangan ang BSP kung ipagpapatuloy pa nila ang mahigpit na patakaran sa interes.
ALEX: Ibig sabihin: because of the weak GDP print, the BSP is in a state of hesitation over whether to continue its tight interest-rate policy.
SAM: Ang pagkakaiba ng "pag-aalinlangan" sa mas kaswal na "medyo nag-aalangan" — ang una ay isang mas matimbang, mas institusyonal na pag-aatubili, habang ang huli ay parang personal at pang-araw-araw na pagdududa lang.
ALEX: Magandang pares din ito — isang salita tungkol sa katiwalian sa pulitika, isa tungkol sa pag-aalinlangan sa patakarang pang-ekonomiya. Pareho namang tungkol sa mga desisyong ginagawa — o hindi ginagawa — sa likod ng saradong pinto.

## SEGMENT SIX — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
ALEX: Op-eds today — Ben Casselman, the New York Times's chief economics correspondent, published a piece called "AI Is Reshaping the Economy, but the Data Can't Keep Up."
SAM: His steelman is basically intellectual honesty about uncertainty — official government statistics were built for a slower, pre-AI economy, response rates to surveys keep falling, and the agencies collecting this data are themselves underfunded. He points to the Yale Budget Lab's occupational-churn tracker, which so far finds no clear link between AI adoption and job losses — and notably, the firms investing the most in AI are actually hiring faster, not shedding workers.
ALEX: The counter is that waiting for clean, aggregate data to settle the question is itself a choice, and it favors inaction. AI-attributed layoffs at Amazon, Salesforce, Meta, Accenture and IBM topped roughly fifty thousand in a single bad month last October, and companies are increasingly naming AI explicitly in their layoff filings even if economists can't yet prove causation in the topline numbers.
SAM: "Too soon to tell" can be a genuinely honest position, but it can also quietly become a permanent alibi — the data will always lag the disruption it's trying to measure, and by the time it's clean enough to be certain, the disruption's already happened to the people it happened to.
ALEX: Second piece — Liam Denning at Bloomberg Opinion, "When Texas Cools on AI Data Centers, Big Tech Has a Problem," published yesterday.
SAM: His steelman ties directly into our energy segment today. Governor Abbott stood on a stage with Alphabet's Sundar Pichai less than a year ago, calling Texas the epicenter of AI development — and now he's visibly cooler on the buildout, as grid strain, local opposition and ratepayer anger have all sharpened. Denning's point is that if even the most AI-friendly state in the country is hedging, that's a leading indicator for how hyperscalers should be pricing political risk everywhere else.
ALEX: The counter is that local friction in one or two counties isn't the same thing as statewide policy risk. ERCOT's market structure and a genuinely pro-growth legislature both have strong incentives to keep courting hyperscaler tax revenue, and the actual grid buildout — the turbines, the transformers, the interconnection fights we just covered — keeps proceeding regardless of one governor's rhetorical mood.
SAM: Read those two together with everything else in today's show, and there's a real pattern — the AI story keeps getting harder to cleanly measure or cleanly predict, on the labor-market side and the political-support side both, exactly while the physical buildout underneath it keeps moving forward on its own momentum regardless.
ALEX: Which might be the most honest one-line summary of where this cycle actually is right now — nobody can prove the disruption yet, nobody can stop the buildout yet, and both of those things are true for the same underlying reason: reality's simply moving faster than anyone's ability to measure or govern it.
