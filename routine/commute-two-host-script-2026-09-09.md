## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

SAM: Two Mandarin words before China — one's a formal connective, one's an economic term specifically for today's inflation data.

ALEX: First: 甚至 — shènzhì. It means "even" or "to the extent that" — an escalating adverb you drop in front of the most extreme example in a list, to say things went further than you'd expect. It's a mixed register, common in both spoken analysis and formal writing, and it's exactly the word for building an argument that's getting worse as it goes.

SAM: Example sentence?

ALEX: 消费需求持续低迷，制造业利润甚至出现下滑。 ("Consumer demand continues to be sluggish; manufacturing profits have even declined.") Notice the structure — 甚至 sits right before the extreme case, flagging that what follows tops everything said before it.

SAM: Second word, and this one's specific to today's China numbers.

ALEX: 输入性通胀 — shūrùxìng tōngzhàng. It literally breaks down as "imported" plus "inflation" — a fixed economic term for inflation driven by rising costs from outside your own economy, like global oil or commodity prices, rather than genuine domestic demand.

SAM: How's that different from just saying 通货膨胀, regular inflation?

ALEX: 通货膨胀 is the general word for inflation, no matter the cause. 输入性通胀 specifically diagnoses the source as external — it's the term an economist reaches for when they want to say "prices are rising, but it's not because people are spending more, it's because the cost of what we're importing went up." That distinction is exactly the argument in today's China data.

SAM: Combine both words the way you'd actually hear it on a finance desk?

ALEX: 输入性通胀掩盖了内需疲软的问题，情况甚至比数据表面显示的更严重。 ("Imported inflation has masked the problem of weak domestic demand — the situation is even more serious than what the data shows on the surface.") That's essentially the sentence we're about to say about China, just with the numbers attached.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY

SAM: Op-ed today ties right back into the market segment — the Washington Post Editorial Board on deficits as a security threat.

ALEX: Their piece, published Sunday, argues the deficit is quote "the biggest threat to U.S. national security" — not through the usual inflation or crowding-out lens, but through military readiness. Their case: real national defense depends on having fiscal headroom to borrow massively when an actual war hits. By running today's deficits at this scale during relative peacetime, Washington's already spent the borrowing capacity that should be held in reserve for a real contingency — a Taiwan invasion, a NATO Article 5 call, a prolonged Mideast conflict like the one we opened with today.

SAM: They're echoing an old warning there, right?

ALEX: Directly — Admiral Mullen's 2010 line that quote "the most significant threat to our national security is our debt." The Post's sharper version: judging by deficit size alone, quote, "one might assume the U.S. is already fighting a war" — leaving nothing in reserve if a real one starts.

SAM: What's the strongest pushback on that?

ALEX: That this exact alarm has been sounded every few years since at least 2010, and the US absorbed COVID-scale and GFC-scale emergency borrowing without the Treasury market seizing up — because reserve-currency status and market depth give far more slack than a household-budget analogy implies. The sharper counter is that the actual binding constraint is inflation, not deficit size on its own, and that a lot of today's "peacetime" spending — Ukraine aid, Indo-Pacific deterrence buildup — is already quasi-wartime, which undercuts the clean peace-versus-war binary the whole argument leans on.

SAM: Second piece, and it's a domestic-politics paradox — union approval numbers.

ALEX: Also Washington Post Opinion — arguing that record union approval, 71%, Gallup's highest since 1965, is being misread as a mandate for aggressive unionization. When people are actually asked whether they want to personally join one, most say no — 82% back the core right-to-work principle that no one should be compelled to join, and it's 64 to 32 against mandatory dues.

SAM: What's their analogy for that gap?

ALEX: They compare it to approving of the fire department without wanting to be a firefighter yourself — liking that a countervailing institution exists isn't the same as wanting personal membership in it. The pushback, though, is real: collapsing "wouldn't personally join" into "doesn't want unions to grow" ignores that most non-union workers have simply never had a genuinely fair shot at organizing — weak NLRB enforcement and employer intimidation are a supply problem, not a preference problem. And separately, Axios and Gallup found a record 47% actually want unions to have more influence, which is rising demand for collective power even while the personal "would you join" number lags behind it.
