## SEGMENT ONE — CHINA
ALEX: China's story today is the direct cause of that Korea crash, and it's worth being precise about what actually happened, because it's easy to overstate.
SAM: Go.
ALEX: A Shanghai state-backed company has begun mass production of China's first homegrown immersion DUV lithography machines — roughly five units delivered so far this year to SMIC, Hua Hong, and CXMT, with about twenty more slated for 2027. DUV lithography is the equipment tier that's critical for chips down to about 7 nanometers — the workhorse node for a huge share of the world's actual chip volume, well below the absolute cutting edge but enormously consequential in scale.
SAM: And the honest caveat?
ALEX: The platform is still viewed as roughly a generation behind ASML on throughput, overlay precision, and reliability — this is not "China has caught up." But it is China's first real domestic mass-production milestone in a category it has never before produced at scale, and ASML shares slid on the report. The read that matters: this is the equipment side, not just the chip side, of what we've been calling China Shock 3.0. Beijing isn't just subsidizing memory output anymore — it's now shipping the tools that make chips, which is the harder, longer-horizon part of import substitution. That's why Korea, which sells both the memory and increasingly competes on the equipment side, took the sharper hit than anyone in the US chip complex did overnight.
SAM: The tools, not just the chips. Onto power.

## SEGMENT TWO — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
ALEX: This is where sovereign capital and grid physics collide, and the lead story today is a harder-edged sibling to the NextEra-Dominion merger we've mentioned before.
SAM: Harder-edged how?
ALEX: NextEra is still negotiating final agreements with both the US and Japanese governments — part of the broader 550 billion dollar Japan trade package — for 9.5 gigawatts of gas-fired data center "hubs." One is 5.2 gigawatts and 16 billion dollars in Anderson County, Texas. The other is 4.3 gigawatts and 17 billion dollars in Pennsylvania. These talks were supposed to close by this spring. They're still open.
SAM: So sovereign capital is now a bottleneck on gigawatts, the same way turbine supply and interconnection queues are.
ALEX: Precisely — this is one step past Chevron's Project Kilby, where an oil major became a power developer. Here it's a foreign government's trade commitment gating whether a specific gas hub gets built at all. And the merger itself has news too: Virginia's State Corporation Commission has scheduled its first public hearing for November, and NextEra executives are now guiding to closing the Dominion deal "by late 2027" — pushed back from the original second-half-2027 target. Filed with FERC, the NRC, and three state regulators, and still not close to done.
SAM: There's also a governance fight brewing inside PJM that sounds bigger than the usual cost-allocation squabbles.
ALEX: It is bigger. FERC Chair Laura Swett ran a technical conference on July 23rd and effectively gave PJM until the end of September to reform itself — board independence, a real formal role for states in filing rights — or FERC imposes the reforms unilaterally. And here's the tell that this is serious: some transmission owners inside PJM are now openly discussing leaving the RTO altogether. That's not a rate case, that's a legitimacy crisis for the market structure that clears more data-center interconnection requests than anywhere else in the country.
SAM: Give me the number that actually quantifies how tight this is getting.
ALEX: SemiAnalysis has the sharpest one. Behind-the-meter data center deployment — generation built on-site, skipping the interconnection queue entirely — is on pace to cross 50% of new US data center capacity by 2028, with the behind-the-meter total addressable market topping 50 gigawatts a year by 2029. But gas turbine additions stay under 10 gigawatts a year through 2026 and 2027. And here's the number that should worry anyone underwriting PJM load: the 2027-2028 capacity auction cleared 134,478 megawatts of unforced capacity against a 20% target reserve margin — the actual reserve margin is 14.4%. That's a physical deficit of roughly 6,517 megawatts, before the next wave of data center load even shows up.
SAM: Any names attached to that behind-the-meter wave?
ALEX: Three concrete ones worth knowing. Crusoe's Goodnight site in ERCOT — 525.5 megawatts, building toward a roughly 1-gigawatt IT campus. AWS at Comanche Peak — a 1,200-megawatt deal, a 20-year power purchase agreement ramping through 2032. And CyrusOne with Constellation at Freestone — up to 760 megawatts potential. Behind-the-meter isn't a side case anymore, it's becoming the primary path for the biggest loads, precisely because the interconnection queue and that shrinking reserve margin make the grid-connected path slower and riskier.
SAM: There's also a legal ruling this week that matters for anyone underwriting federal co-investment in this build-out.
ALEX: Right — a federal judge, Amit Mehta, ruled that the Department of Energy's cancellation of 7.6 billion dollars in clean-energy grants across sixteen states — battery plants, hydrogen, grid upgrades, carbon capture — was based, quote, "solely on political identity," a violation of equal protection. DOE actually admitted the political motive in court filings after months of denying it publicly. That's a real regulatory-risk data point: if you're underwriting a project counting on a federal grant, the political durability of that grant just got tested in court, and lost.
SAM: One more before we move on — a rate case that's a clean illustration of the affordability-versus-return fight utilities are having everywhere right now.
ALEX: Pepco, the DC-area utility owned by Exelon, is asking Maryland regulators to raise its allowed return on equity to 10.5% from 9.5%. Maryland's People's Counsel — the state's consumer advocate — is alleging the ask is inflated through what's called "double leveraging," essentially borrowing at the parent-company level and then asking ratepayers to fund a higher equity return than the underlying economics justify. The Public Service Commission's decision is due in early August.
SAM: A hundred basis points doesn't sound like much.
ALEX: On a rate base the size of Pepco's, it's real money flowing straight into monthly bills, at the exact moment every one of these utilities is also asking customers to fund gigawatts of new data center interconnection. That's the tension underneath every one of tonight's stories — the capital is there for the build-out, but who actually pays the return on it is being litigated state by state, case by case.
SAM: Sovereign capital, grid governance, a reserve-margin deficit, a court ruling on federal grant risk, and now a return-on-equity fight in Maryland — a full week of energy news packed into one segment. Let's head east.

SAM: And BSP Governor Remolona actually spoke to reporters today — what did he say?

But the sharper line was about Friday's record print itself — he called the 61.85 number "misleading," arguing it reflects broad dollar strength against basically every currency, not a peso-specific weakness. SAM: Is that just a governor managing the narrative, or is there a real distinction there?
ALEX: There's a real distinction, and it matters for what BSP does next. If the move is peso-specific — capital flight, a current-account problem — that argues for a rate response. If it's dollar-broad — every currency moving together on US rate expectations or safe-haven flows — the correct response is closer to what he described: light intervention, patience, no hike. And that's exactly what he signaled — he sees only a "small chance" of aggressive or off-cycle tightening. The one thing he did concede, and it's the honest part: continued peso weakness still stokes inflation through import pass-through, since most of what the Philippines imports is dollar-invoiced. So he's threading a needle — don't overreact to a print he thinks is noise, but don't ignore that weakness itself is an inflation input regardless of its cause.

## SEGMENT FOUR — VOCAB OF THE DAY
SAM: 到了学中文的时间了。
ALEX: 没错。今天有两个词，都跟今天的新闻有关系，我们直接开始，用中文聊。
SAM: 好，你先来第一个。
ALEX: 第一个是一个连接词，书面语，很正式：不仅，而且。今天英伟达的新闻正好可以用这个词。
SAM: 那你造个句子。
ALEX: 英伟达这次不仅要为OpenAI的巨额资本支出提供担保，而且还要资助他们购买自己的芯片。
SAM: 意思是说，英伟达不但是担保人，而且还是资金方，两个角色都占了。这跟口语里常用的"不但，而且"有什么区别？
ALEX: 意思基本一样，但是"不仅，而且"更正式，更书面，常出现在分析报告或者新闻评论里。而且它常常表示后面这件事比前面更让人意外，或者程度更深。你看我这句话，前半句已经很惊人了——担保两百五十亿美元的租金；后半句更狠——还要资助三千五百亿美元让对方买自己的芯片。
SAM: 明白了，"不仅，而且"是往上加码，不是简单地并列。
ALEX: 对，这就是它的用法。
SAM: 好，我来说第二个词，也是今天新闻里的：施压。
ALEX: 这个词很常见，尤其在政治和外交新闻里。
SAM: 我用今天参议院的新闻造句：参议院正准备立法，向购买俄罗斯石油的国家施压。
ALEX: 说得好。"施压"字面意思是"施加压力"，但是要注意，它跟"逼迫"不一样。
SAM: 有什么区别？
ALEX: "逼迫"是直接强迫，对方没有选择的余地。"施压"是从外部施加压力，但是被施压的一方还是可以选择抵抗，或者不理会。参议院对中国和印度施压，但这两个国家完全可以不理，只是要承担后果。搭配也要记住："向"加上对象，再加"施压"——向某人施压，向某国施压。
SAM: 从担保到施压，两个很有分量的词。谢谢，Alex，中文部分就到这里。
ALEX: Now let's switch — Sam, take us into Tagalog.
SAM: Sige, dalawang salita ngayon, pareho galing sa balitang Pilipinas kanina. Simulan natin sa "katatagan."
ALEX: Sabihin mo sa akin kung paano ito ginamit.
SAM: Pinapanatili ng BSP ang katatagan ng piso sa pamamagitan ng maliit na interbensyon, hindi agresibong pagtaas ng interes.
ALEX: Ibig sabihin, hindi sila nag-aabang ng lakas, kundi ng katatagan — dalawang magkaibang bagay.
SAM: Tama. "Katatagan" ay hindi pareho ng "lakas." Ang lakas ay tungkol sa kung gaano kalakas, kung gaano ka-mataas ang halaga. Ang katatagan ay tungkol sa kung gaano ito kacontrolado, kung gaano ito kastable — kahit humihina nang dahan-dahan, pwede pa ring may katatagan, basta't kontrolado ang paghina. Galing ito sa salitang-ugat na "tatag," ibig sabihin matatag, hindi natitinag.
ALEX: Kaya naiintindihan natin bakit ganoon ang sinabi ni Gobernador Remolona — hindi niya sinasabing malakas ang piso, sinasabi niyang kontrolado ang sitwasyon.
SAM: Eksakto. At ito naman ang pangalawang salita, direktang mula sa sinabi niya: "nakaliligaw."
ALEX: Gamitin natin sa pangungusap.
SAM: Sabi ni Gobernador Remolona, nakaliligaw ang numerong iyon dahil sa pangkalahatang lakas ng dolyar, hindi partikular na kahinaan ng piso.
ALEX: Ano ang pinagkaiba nito sa "peke"?
SAM: Malaking pagkakaiba. Ang "peke" ay nangangahulugang huwad — parang may sadyang pandaraya, hindi totoo. Pero ang "nakaliligaw" ay galing sa salitang "ligaw," ibig sabihin maligaw ng landas. Totoo ang numero — 61.85 talaga ang naitala — pero ang punto ni Remolona ay hindi ito dapat basahin bilang isang kuwento tungkol sa piso lamang, dahil ililigaw ka nito sa maling konklusyon kung ganoon ang unawain mo.
ALEX: So it's real, but it points you the wrong way.
SAM: Exactly — "nakaliligaw" is about interpretation, not fraud. Katatagan and nakaliligaw, both straight from Remolona's own briefing today.
ALEX: A formal connective and a hard verb from the Mandarin side, a policy word and a framing word from the Tagalog side — four ways to sound sharper in either language today.
