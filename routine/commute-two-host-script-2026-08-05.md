## SEGMENT ONE — HEADLINES

ALEX: Russia hit Kyiv and the surrounding region overnight with one of the heaviest barrages we've tracked in weeks — 24 ballistic missiles, 4 Zircon or Oniks hypersonic missiles, and 115 drones. Zelensky called it a heavy strike. At least 17 people are dead, dozens wounded.

ALEX: No — a brewery, hardware-store depots, a railway station. Logistics and warehousing, not the front line. SAM: So we've moved from strikes on energy infrastructure to strikes on e-commerce warehouses.

SAM: Meanwhile, on the other conflict we track closely — Hormuz — there's actually a genuinely new development, and it's the opposite direction.

ALEX: Axios is reporting this morning that the US, Iran, and Oman are closing in on an interim deal to reopen the Strait, with an announcement targeted for today. The draft terms are specific — a 60-day arrangement, extendable, with inbound Gulf traffic routed through a northern lane in Iranian waters and outbound traffic through a southern lane in Omani waters, coordinated with Iran. No tolls or transit fees during the 60 days. And naval mines cleared from the median shipping lane within 30 days.

SAM: That's a real mechanism, not just a headline about talks resuming.

ALEX: Right — this is the first time we've seen concrete lane geography and a timeline attached, rather than just "talks continue" or, as we had a week ago, Iran flatly denying any framework exists. The goal on the US side is restarting the ceasefire and reopening nuclear talks. Worth watching whether Tehran actually confirms it today.

SAM: Remind me why a two-lane, non-toll arrangement is actually workable, rather than just a face-saving gesture.

ALEX: Because it solves the sovereignty problem for both sides without anyone formally ceding control. Iran keeps the northern lane inside its own waters, which lets Tehran claim it never surrendered oversight of the strait. The US and Gulf shippers get a southern lane inside Omani waters, which they trust isn't going to be a chokepoint for harassment. And Oman, as mediator, is holding both ends together — the same broker role it played in the earlier user-fee proposal Iran rejected a few weeks back. The difference this time is nobody's trying to charge a toll, which was the sticking point before.

SAM: So — Ukraine escalating, Hormuz potentially de-escalating, in the same 24 hours. Let's get to what markets did with all of that.

## SEGMENT ONE — U.S. BUSINESS

ALEX: We've essentially covered the first one — AMD's beat is the story of the week in chips, full stop. $1.66 EPS, $11.536 billion in revenue, and it's the print that reset sentiment for the entire semiconductor complex heading into the back half of earnings season.

SAM: Cheaper financing showing up in a completely different corner of the market from where the AMD story is playing out.

ALEX: That's the thing about a rates move across the whole curve — it doesn't stay contained to the sector that caused it.

SAM: And the China number that actually anchors today's whole throughline.

For the first half of 2026, the total surplus is $575.98 billion, actually down slightly from $586 billion a year ago, because imports are now growing faster than exports — a sign Chinese firms are stockpiling AI-adjacent inputs, not just selling finished goods.

SAM: So the chip cycle that drove AMD's stock yesterday is the same one showing up in a Chinese customs release from the other side of the world.

ALEX: One demand curve, two very different data releases. That's the connective tissue for today.

## SEGMENT TWO — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES

SAM: And that demand curve is exactly where we go next — energy and data centers. Utility Dive's Daily Dive from the past two days had four items worth the full segment.

ALEX: Start with Southern Company. Its contracted large-load book just hit 17 gigawatts, and the new driver is a 3.2-gigawatt OpenAI data center near Savannah, Georgia. What's actually notable isn't the size — it's the contract structure. It embeds 1 gigawatt of flexible demand response for peak shaving, and Southern is calling it the first time it's codified a flexibility provision directly into a data-center interconnection contract, rather than negotiating curtailment rights as a bolt-on afterward.

SAM: So the load itself is agreeing, contractually, to be curtailable.

ALEX: Right, and that's the shift the whole sector's been groping toward — instead of treating every gigawatt of AI load as fixed, firm demand that has to be served no matter what, you're building flexibility into the deal at signing. It changes the reserve-margin math on the utility's side, because a gigawatt that can be shed during a peak event doesn't need the same firm capacity backing it as one that can't.

SAM: Meanwhile Exelon is moving the opposite direction on its pipeline.

ALEX: Exelon's "high probability" data-center pipeline just shrank 40%, down to 11 gigawatts. CFO Jeanne Jones was explicit about why — they're using transmission service agreements as a screening filter to weed out speculative projects before they ever get counted as real. It's the demand-side mirror of the AEP turbine-lockup story we covered a couple weeks back — AEP locked up generation supply at the portfolio level; Exelon's doing collateral screening at the individual-project level, on the load side. Same underlying problem — too many speculative megawatts chasing scarce interconnection — attacked from two different points in the system.

SAM: And not every utility is choosing to filter.

ALEX: Xcel Energy is doing the opposite — leaning in. Its 2026-through-2030 base capital plan is $60 billion, and it's now flagging "line of sight" to another $10 billion incremental, explicitly earmarked for transmission and generation tied to data-center load, alongside a 3% retail sales growth guide. So you've got three different utilities running three different playbooks on the exact same AI-load wave — Southern structuring flexibility into contracts, Exelon filtering the pipeline down, Xcel raising capex to chase it.

SAM: And the fourth item is more of a caution flag.

ALEX: It is — Eversource. Q2 income plunged on transmission ROE pressure and offshore-wind charges, and Jefferies flagged that Connecticut Light and Power's proposed $1 billion smart-meter rollout actually pencils out with a negative benefit-to-cost ratio. Useful counterweight to keep in mind — the AI-load story is real, but it's not lifting every utility's earnings. Some are still fighting cost-recovery battles on legacy infrastructure that have nothing to do with data centers at all.

SAM: Three ways to underwrite the same demand wave, plus one utility that's still stuck on a 2010s problem.

ALEX: And if you zoom out, this is the sector maturing in real time. A year ago almost every utility earnings call was some version of "our data-center pipeline doubled" as an unqualified positive. Now the actual differentiation is in how disciplined the underwriting is — whether a utility is pricing curtailment risk into the contract like Southern, screening speculative interconnection requests like Exelon, or just betting the load shows up and raising capex ahead of it like Xcel. That dispersion is exactly what you'd expect once a genuinely new demand category stops being novel and starts being underwritten like any other large industrial customer.

SAM: Underwriting discipline as the actual tell, not the headline gigawatt number.

That's exactly why BSP's inflation read matters as much as the GDP number itself — the two are tangled together through the same import bill, and it's why you're seeing analysts split on whether the response should be one more hike or a pause.

## SEGMENT FOUR — VOCAB OF THE DAY

SAM: Vocab time — two Mandarin words, then two Tagalog, and we're doing the whole thing in-language today.

ALEX: 好，那我们先说中文。今天的第一个词是"既然……就……"，一个表示因果推理的连接词，偏书面语，常用在财经分析里。(Alright, let's start in Mandarin. Today's first word is "既然……就……" — a connective for logical cause-and-effect, leaning formal, common in financial and economic analysis.)

SAM: 能不能造个跟今天新闻有关的句子？(Can you make a sentence tied to today's news?)

ALEX: 当然。"既然芯片需求依然旺盛，中国的出口就持续创出新高。"(Of course — "Since chip demand remains strong, China's exports keep hitting new record highs.") 这个句型跟"如果……就……"不一样——"如果"是假设，还没发生；"既然"是已经确定的事实，说话人在这个事实的基础上得出结论。所以在财经报道里，"既然"听起来更有把握，更权威。(This pattern is different from "如果……就……" — "如果" is hypothetical, something that hasn't happened yet; "既然" is an already-established fact, and the speaker is drawing a conclusion from that fact. So in financial reporting, "既然" sounds more assured, more authoritative.)

SAM: 明白了——"既然"是先摆事实,再讲结论。那第二个词呢？(Got it — "既然" lays out the fact first, then the conclusion. What's the second word?)

ALEX: 第二个词是"打破"，意思是打破一个纪录或者一个僵局。"标普和道琼斯昨天同时打破了历史纪录。"(The second word is "打破" — to break, as in breaking a record or a deadlock. "The S&P and the Dow simultaneously broke their all-time record highs yesterday.") "打破"常常跟"纪录"搭配，也可以说"打破僵局"、"打破平衡"。它的近义词是"突破"，但"突破"更强调超越一个门槛或者限制，比如技术上的突破,而"打破"更强调把原来的状态彻底改变,比如打破纪录、打破规矩。两个词口语和书面语都能用,但"突破"在科技和分析语境里更常见。(“打破” often pairs with “纪录” — “record” — and you can also say “打破僵局,” break a deadlock, or “打破平衡,” break a balance. Its near-synonym is “突破,” but “突破” emphasizes surpassing a threshold or limit — like a technological breakthrough — while “打破” emphasizes completely changing an existing state, like breaking a record or breaking the rules. Both words work in speech and in writing, but “突破” shows up more often in tech and analytical contexts.)

SAM: 有意思——两个词都是"打破"什么东西,但用法完全不一样。(Interesting — both words are about breaking something, but the usage is completely different.)

ALEX: 没错。(Exactly.)

SAM: Ngayon naman, Tagalog. Ang unang salita natin ngayon ay "lumakas" — para sa bagyong Maymay, na unti-unting lumalakas habang papalapit sa Ilocos. (Now, Tagalog. Our first word today is "lumakas" — for Storm Maymay, which is gradually strengthening as it nears Ilocos.)

ALEX: Kabaligtaran ito ng "humina," na naituro na natin dati. "Humina" ay unti-unting pagkawala ng lakas — parang isang bagyo na nawawalan ng puwersa. Pero "lumakas" ay ang kabaligtaran — dumarami ang lakas, tumataas ang bilis ng hangin. (This is the opposite of "humina," which we already taught before. "Humina" is a gradual loss of strength — like a storm losing its power. But "lumakas" is the opposite — strength increasing, wind speed rising.)

SAM: Kaya kapag pinagsama mo ang dalawa, naiintindihan mo agad ang buong buhay ng isang bagyo — nagsisimula, lumalakas, tapos humihina. Ganito rin ba ginagamit ang salita sa pang-araw-araw na usapan, hindi lang sa panahon? (So when you put the two together, you immediately understand a storm's whole life cycle — starts, strengthens, then weakens. Is the word also used this way in everyday conversation, not just for weather?)

ALEX: Oo, talaga — pwede mo ring sabihin na "lumakas ang tugtugan" o "lumakas ang boses niya." Pero sa balitang pang-ekonomiya o panahon, mas malinaw at mas pormal ang gamit nito kumpara sa mas kolokyal na "tumindi." (Yes, definitely — you can also say the music "got louder" or their voice "got stronger." But in economic or weather reporting, its usage is clearer and more formal compared to the more colloquial "tumindi.")

SAM: Ang pangalawang salita naman ay "salungat" — para sa dalawang magkaibang hula tungkol sa BSP ngayong Agosto 27. (Our second word is "salungat" — meaning conflicting or contrary — for the two different forecasts about BSP this August 27.)

ALEX: "Salungat ang dalawang hula — sabi ng isang analyst, isa pang hike; sabi naman ng isang balita, titigil na ang BSP sa pagtaas ng interes." ("The two forecasts are conflicting — one analyst says one more hike; but another report says BSP will pause its rate hikes.") Mas malakas ang "salungat" kumpara sa payak na "magkaiba." Ang "magkaiba" ay simpleng pagkakaiba lang — parang magkaibang kulay. Pero ang "salungat" ay may bahid ng tunggalian — dalawang bagay na hindi maaaring pareho ang tama, magkasalungat na direksyon. Mas pormal ito, madalas gamitin sa pagsusuri o debate. ("Salungat" is stronger than the plain word "magkaiba." "Magkaiba" is just a simple difference — like different colors. But "salungat" carries a sense of conflict — two things that can't both be right, opposing directions. It's more formal, often used in analysis or debate.)

SAM: Kaya kapag may dalawang eksperto na hindi magkasundo, mas tamang salita ang "salungat" kaysa "magkaiba." (So when two experts don't agree, "salungat" is the more accurate word than "magkaiba.")

ALEX: Tama — at ganoon nga ang nangyayari ngayon sa BSP.
