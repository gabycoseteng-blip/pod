## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: For the deep dive, we're leaving markets and energy policy behind and going to the Supreme Court, because the new term opens Monday with a case that could decide who pays for climate damage in America. This is drawn from open reporting, Bloomberg Law, Reuters, SCOTUSblog, and Colorado Public Radio, rather than a single feature, and I'll flag where the reporting is thin.
ALEX: The case is Suncor Energy versus the Board of County Commissioners of Boulder County, with Exxon as a co-petitioner. Boulder sued in state court, using public and private nuisance, trespass, unjust enrichment, and civil conspiracy, arguing that the companies knew their products would warm the planet and misled the public about it. The Colorado Supreme Court let the case proceed 5 to 2 in May of 2025, the companies petitioned in August, and the justices granted review on February 23rd.
SAM: The question presented is whether federal law precludes state-law claims for injuries allegedly caused by interstate and international greenhouse gas emissions. The companies say it does. Their line is that this is emissions regulation dressed up as tort law, that the Clean Air Act and federal foreign policy own the field, and that allowing it means 50 states setting climate policy through juries.
ALEX: And the stakes are what make it a big case. Exxon and Suncor told the justices that nearly 60 state and local governments have filed similar suits seeking billions of dollars. BP, Chevron, ConocoPhillips, Shell, and Phillips 66 have been sued in comparable cases. The Trump administration is siding with the companies, arguing that federal law forecloses Boulder's claims.
SAM: Now the twist. Justice Alito recused himself, by a one-line letter from the clerk, less than a week before argument and with no explanation. He later told Bloomberg that, taking into account the particular arguments, recusal was the prudent step and a difficult judgement call, and he said the Court's legal office had told him he was not required to recuse.
ALEX: His financial disclosure shows no Exxon or Suncor holdings, but about two dozen stocks including Phillips 66 and ConocoPhillips, which are named in sibling suits. There's also reporting about family energy-related inheritance. I'd treat that last part carefully, it comes from advocacy-adjacent coverage, but the basic point is that the recusal itself is unexplained and the arithmetic is what matters.
SAM: Because with 8 justices, a 4 to 4 tie affirms the Colorado ruling without setting national precedent. That's the second-order read. A tie is a win for Boulder, since its case continues, but it is no nationwide shield for the companies and no nationwide loss either. The other 59-odd cases would keep going in their own states with the same unresolved question hanging over them.
ALEX: What's contested is the legal doctrine. SCOTUSblog describes preemption law in this area as very confused, and Legal Planet has written a guide for the lost. The companies say these are really claims about global emissions, which are federal by nature. Boulder, and groups like Just Security, say these are consumer-deception and nuisance claims about what the companies knew and said, not about regulating emissions, and that nothing in the Clean Air Act removes that traditional state power.
SAM: There's a procedural subtlety underneath that. In a tort case the remedy is damages from specific defendants, and the companies' argument is that the damages are a function of worldwide emissions from everyone. A court has to decide whether the harm can be traced to a defendant's conduct at all, or whether it's too diffuse for any court, which is essentially a justiciability argument in a preemption costume.
ALEX: For this audience, the energy read matters. A clean company win could end about 60 suits and remove a long-tail liability from oil and gas balance sheets, which is relevant to how lenders and utilities think about the gas-fired buildout for data centers. A tie leaves the liability overhang alive. And because Alito's recusal makes a tie more likely than the cert grant implied, the market should probably put more weight on the overhang staying.
SAM: And the timeline is slow. Argument is Monday, October 5th, and a decision doesn't come before 2027, with the term ending next June. So for lenders and insurers, the practical effect for the next 9 months is uncertainty rather than resolution.
ALEX: What I'd watch on Monday is how the questions split. If the conservative justices press Boulder on federalism and the liberal justices press the companies on why state tort law gets pre-empted by a statute that never mentions it, the tie becomes the base case, and that's when oral argument stops being theatre and starts being a forecast.
SAM: It's one of 7 cases in the first two weeks of the term, alongside immigration detention without bond hearings, veterans' benefits, federal sentencing, and digital privacy. But this is the one that sets the tone, and the Court chose to put it first.
ALEX: One caveat on sourcing, which is that the family-wealth details are secondary reporting, and I'd rather leave them as a question than a fact.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: Starting with MISO, which is trying to fix the thing every large-load developer complains about, serial studies. Its proposed Large Load Addition Resource Study would evaluate loads of 250 MW or more together with their contracted generation, in parallel, rather than in series, and target transmission approval plus a generator interconnection agreement in 120 days, against 180 or more today.
ALEX: The design is modelled on its expedited resource addition study. There would be 3 cycles a year, capped at 10 load-and-generation pairs per cycle. To get in you need a firm offtake agreement, 100% site control, and the load and generator in the same local resource zone. Request size runs from 250 MW up to 150% of the load's peak. First agreements are possible in 2027.
SAM: The timing is worth marking. It was proposed on August 27th at the large load working group, MidAmerican asked to cut the threshold from 250 to 200 MW, comments run through early October, a final proposal is planned for October 29th, and the FERC filing is slated for November. Separately, MISO filed large load interconnection reliability tariff revisions on August 28th, requesting an October 18th effective date.
ALEX: Context: FERC says MISO is the fastest-growing data center region, with a 43% compound growth rate in data center capacity since 2020. So this is a process built for a queue that has already arrived, and the requirement to bring your own generation is the real policy bet, because it makes the large load a co-developer of supply instead of a claimant on existing supply.
SAM: Next, Pennsylvania. The Public Utility Commission voted Thursday on a tentative order that would let new large loads without newly secured capacity be curtailed ahead of other customers in emergencies. Utilities couldn't classify large loads as critical users exempt from pre-emergency or emergency cuts, and they'd keep a registry of major users to notify during what the order calls an Interim Resource event. It's out for comment.
ALEX: The vote count was reported as unanimous, though accounts differ on whether it was 4 to 0 or 5 to 0. The backing study says PJM's reliability standards fail from 2027 through 2030 in the reference scenario. That is the central fact: when the regulator's own study says the standard is missed, the question becomes who gets cut first, and Pennsylvania is answering it with a priority list.
SAM: New York has a version of that arithmetic. NYISO's 2026 reliability needs assessment finds the base case 200 MW short by 2033 and 1,800 MW short by 2036. The drivers are 2,700 MW of new large load by 2036, 517 MW of NYPA small gas plants retiring, and about 2,600 MW of aging-generation risk.
ALEX: The queue growth is the striking part, 6 projects and 1,045 MW in 2022, up to 51 projects and 12,670 MW by May of this year. NYISO's proposed process would add higher readiness obligations, a bulk electric large load study, and a non-firm withdrawal service for loads that don't bring generation. Non-firm service is the phrase to remember, because it's the commercial form of a curtailment priority.
SAM: And the hardware. Siemens Energy's gas turbine backlog is around 69 to 70 GW inside a total backlog of 162 billion euros, and its grid technologies backlog is a record 51 billion euros, driven by large power transformers. Mitsubishi Heavy reports a 35 GW large-frame backlog, up from 23 GW a year earlier, with the latest orders for delivery in 2028 through 2030.
ALEX: Those backlog figures come from recent quarterly reports rather than this week, but a Washington Times piece on September 30th made the same point with the headline about the machine the AI boom can't get enough of. The transformer side is just as tight, with lead times for the largest units running 3 to 5 years, against 24 to 30 months before the boom, and an average US lead time around 128 weeks. Hitachi Energy is investing more than $1 billion in the US, including a plant in South Boston, Virginia, due online in 2028.
SAM: And ERCOT's large-load queue was 410 GW as of April 1st, about 87% data centers, against 63 GW in December of 2024. A more recent figure of 466 GW has circulated, but I couldn't confirm the date, so I'll stick with 410.
SAM: For a developer, the practical question is what each of these does to a pro forma. MISO's parallel study shortens the schedule but demands a firm offtake and 100% site control up front, so it filters out speculative requests. Pennsylvania's order makes interruptible status a financing term, because a lender will want to know whether a campus sits at the front or the back of the curtailment line.
ALEX: Exactly. If non-firm service becomes the norm in New York and curtailment priority becomes the norm in PJM, then on-site generation and storage stop being a nice extra and become the thing that converts a conditional interconnection into a bankable one. That's the second-order read on all three of these filings.
ALEX: The pattern across all four items is the same: the queue is outgrowing the grid, regulators are building priority lists, and the equipment that would close the gap is spoken for until 2028. The scarce asset is neither the chip nor the site, it's the firm interconnection.

## SEGMENT FIVE — TAGALOG VOCAB
ALEX: Ngayong umaga, dalawang salita na maririnig natin sa balita mula sa Pilipinas. Ang una ay "pagkabahala."
SAM: Ang "pagkabahala" ay ang pakiramdam ng pag-aalala o pagkabalisa dahil sa isang bagay na maaaring mangyari. Galing ito sa salitang-ugat na "bahala," pero iba na ang dating nito. Ang "bahala ka" ay pagpapaubaya, samantalang ang "pagkabahala" ay may kaba at pangamba na kasama.
ALEX: Pormal ito at madalas mong mababasa sa pahayagan. Halimbawa, "Lumalalim ang pagkabahala ng mga mamumuhunan sa humihinang piso." Hindi ito ginagamit sa karaniwang kuwentuhan. Sa barkada, mas natural ang "kinakabahan ako" o "nag-aalala ako."
SAM: Magkaiba rin ito sa "takot." Ang takot ay matindi at agad, parang may humahabol sa iyo. Ang pagkabahala ay mabagal na pag-ipon ng alalahanin, parang ulap na lumalapot bago ang ulan. Kaya bagay ito sa mga balita tungkol sa ekonomiya.
ALEX: Ang pangalawang salita ay "pag-urong." Ito ay ang pag-atras o pagliit ng isang bagay, lalo na ng produksyon o ng ekonomiya. Halimbawa, "Ang pag-urong ng sektor ng pagmamanupaktura ay unang naitala mula Abril."
SAM: Galing ito sa "urong," na literal na paggalaw pabalik. Kapag may nahihiya o natatakot, sinasabi nating "umurong ang dila." Pero sa balita, ang "pag-urong" ay pormal at teknikal, katumbas ng pagbaba ng antas ng gawain sa ekonomiya.
ALEX: Huwag itong ihalo sa "pagbaba." Ang pagbaba ay simpleng paglugi o pagbagsak ng numero. Ang pag-urong ay may diwa ng pagbabalik sa mas maliit na sukat, kaya mas mabigat itong pakinggan kapag tungkol sa buong sektor.
SAM: Kapag pinagsama mo ang dalawa, "May pagkabahala sa posibleng pag-urong ng ekonomiya." Iyan ang pangungusap na makikita mo sa mga pahayagan ngayong linggo.
ALEX: Pakinggan natin ang dalawang salitang iyan sa balita ngayon.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
ALEX: Two Mandarin words today, both from the China story coming up. The first is a connective, 固然, used in the pattern 固然……但是. It concedes that something is true, then pivots to the point you actually want to make.
SAM: The register is written and editorial, 书面语, the kind of thing you read in a financial commentary. Here's an example. 假期出行人次固然创下纪录，但消费依然谨慎。(Holiday trips have indeed hit a record, but spending is still cautious.)
ALEX: The contrast to know is 虽然……但是, which you've already learned. 虽然 is a neutral concession. 固然 sounds more like an argument, as though the writer is saying, yes, I grant you that, and now here's why it doesn't matter.
SAM: The second word is 转化为, meaning convert into or translate into. It's an abstract collocation, and the standard pairings are 客流转化为消费 and 流量转化为销量, meaning foot traffic turning into spending, and web traffic turning into sales.
ALEX: Example. 庞大的客流并未转化为相应的消费。(The huge flow of visitors did not translate into a corresponding level of spending.) Notice 并未, a formal negation, which fits with 转化为 in the written register.
SAM: Compare it with 变成, which is the plain spoken word for turn into. 变成 is just a change of state. 转化为 implies a process or mechanism, something working through a funnel, which is why it fits business writing.
ALEX: Another one, 这家公司努力把游客转化为长期顾客。(This company is working hard to convert tourists into long-term customers.) The verb 转化 usually takes 为, and a person or a process is the thing doing the converting.
SAM: Last one for 固然. 市场固然有机会，但风险也不能忽视。(There are indeed opportunities in the market, but the risks can't be ignored either.) We'll hear both of these in the China story.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
ALEX: Paul Nakasone, the former head of the NSA and Cyber Command who sits on OpenAI's board, argues in the Washington Post that AI labs must partner with the government to secure critical infrastructure before it's too late. The steelman is strong: frontier models are cyber-offense multipliers, the labs hold the models, the government holds the threat intelligence and the authorities, and only a standing partnership can patch the grid and the water systems before attackers weaponise them.
SAM: The counter-take starts with the byline. The author sits on OpenAI's board, so the partnership he proposes hands incumbents a seat in writing the rules. Second, a national-security agency can't verify what a lab says about its own models. And it skips the utility angle entirely: grid operators are under-resourced and need mandates and funding, not a lab-and-government club.
ALEX: A second piece, a Washington Post opinion on the roadless rule, argues it has flaws but that repealing it entirely creates new ones. The steelman is that 45 million acres of national forest are roadless, which makes fuel reduction and firefighting access hard, and that Mill City, Oregon, after the 2020 Beachie Creek fire, shows how expensive aging roads are to maintain.
SAM: The counter is that wholesale repeal opens millions of acres to new roads the Forest Service already can't afford to maintain, and it hurts drinking-water watersheds and vulnerable species. A scalpel, meaning exemptions for wildfire work, beats a repeal that could take effect as soon as next year.
