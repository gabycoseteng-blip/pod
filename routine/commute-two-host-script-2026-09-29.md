## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

SAM: Alex, for the deep dive we're stepping away from markets and energy entirely. The piece is a peer-reviewed paper published in the New England Journal of Medicine and presented as a late-breaking session at the European Society of Cardiology congress. Its title is "Durability of CRISPR-Cas9 Gene Editing Targeting ANGPTL3 with CTX310." One infusion, one year later, and cholesterol is still down.
ALEX: I want to lay out why this is a big deal, because the headline can sound like ordinary drug-trial news, and it isn't. CTX310 is a CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing therapy from CRISPR Therapeutics, delivered in a single intravenous infusion. It's aimed at the liver, and it knocks out a gene called ANGPTL3.
SAM: Why that gene?
ALEX: Because nature ran the experiment first. People who are born with natural loss-of-function variants in ANGPTL3 have lower triglycerides, lower LDL cholesterol, and lower rates of coronary artery disease, and they appear to be healthy. That is the best kind of drug target, because it is a target validated by human genetics rather than by a hypothesis. There is already an antibody against the same protein, called evinacumab, approved for a rare inherited cholesterol condition. The bet here is that instead of injecting an antibody repeatedly, you edit the gene once.
SAM: Give me the numbers, at the highest dose.
ALEX: At one year, mean reductions from baseline were 79% for the ANGPTL3 protein itself, with a maximum of 89%. Triglycerides fell a mean 48%, up to 78% in the best responder. And LDL cholesterol fell a mean 53%, up to 84%. Company and investigators also report no treatment-related serious adverse events and no grade 3 or higher changes in liver enzymes, which is the standard worry with anything that goes into the liver.
SAM: And the trial is small.
ALEX: It's a phase 1 study, on the order of 15 patients as I've seen it reported, people with hard-to-treat lipid disorders. That is precisely why the durability is the news. In the earlier update, at a couple of months, the question was whether the effect would fade. At one year it hasn't. That is what a permanent edit is supposed to do, and a durable effect is the entire economic case.
SAM: Let's spend a minute on that economic case, because that's what the cardiologists I know keep arguing about. Statins are cheap and they work, but adherence is terrible. A large fraction of patients stop taking them within a year or two.
ALEX: Right, and that's the lever. If a large share of the population at high cardiovascular risk cannot be relied upon to take a daily pill, a one-time intervention that fixes the problem for decades is attractive even at a very high sticker price. The comparison isn't against a $10 generic, it's against decades of cardiovascular events, hospital stays and the drugs you'd otherwise take in combination.
SAM: But the counter-argument writes itself. A phase 1 trial with 15 people tells you about safety and mechanism. It does not tell you that heart attacks go down. LDL is a surrogate endpoint. We believe it's a good one, but the whole history of cardiology is littered with lipid-modifying drugs that moved the number and did not move the outcome.
ALEX: Agreed, and ANGPTL3 in particular is interesting because it lowers triglycerides and LDL through a pathway that is different from the LDL receptor route statins use. The human genetics are supportive, but the outcomes trial does not exist yet.
SAM: And then there's irreversibility. A pill you can stop. An edit you can't.
ALEX: That's the ethics and the regulation both. If a late off-target effect appears at year five, there is no off switch. So regulators will want long follow-up, and I'd expect the label to be restricted, at first, to the people with the worst disease, the ones who have the least to lose. The path from there to the broader population is a question of decades of safety data, not one good year.
SAM: How does the manufacturing side fit? The delivery vehicle here is a lipid nanoparticle, correct?
ALEX: Yes, the same class of delivery technology as the mRNA vaccines. Lipid nanoparticles naturally accumulate in the liver, which is why the liver is where the first generation of in vivo editing therapies are landing. That both explains why cholesterol is such a natural first target and why editing other organs is a much harder problem.
SAM: So what's the second-order read?
ALEX: It's that in vivo gene editing for common diseases stops being a science-fiction category and starts being a capital-allocation category. Investors will ask who else is going after the liver, who has a durable effect, and who can manufacture at scale. And health systems will start modelling what one-off cures do to budgets that were built around chronic-care subscriptions.
SAM: Which is a very odd sentence to say on a morning show, but it's true. The interesting policy fight of the 2030s might be less about what drugs cost and more about how you pay for a treatment whose value arrives over 30 years but whose bill arrives in a single afternoon.
ALEX: Precisely. Pay-over-time and outcomes-based contracts are the obvious answer, and they have been messy in practice. The science just got ahead of the payment system again.
SAM: Where does the principal investigator land?
ALEX: Luke Laffin of the Cleveland Clinic, who discussed the findings, framed it as a proof that a single course can deliver durable lowering. That's a fair way to put it, and it's also a careful one. Proof of durable lowering, not proof of fewer heart attacks.
SAM: A note on how we chose it. We looked for a viral thread or a major essay this week to anchor the deep dive and nothing cleared our bar, so we went with the science. The source is the journal publication and the company's presentation.
ALEX: And if you take one thing away, it's that the honest headline is the boring one: durable, well tolerated so far, tiny sample, outcomes unknown. That's still a remarkable sentence to be able to write about a gene edit in 2026.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

SAM: Time for our Mandarin words of the day, both drawn from the China story we're about to discuss. The first is 出台, chūtái, and it means to roll out or introduce a policy, measure or regulation.
ALEX: The important thing about 出台 is its register. It's formal, the standard verb in official announcements and news reports, and its typical objects are things like 政策, 措施 and 规定, meaning policy, measures and rules. You would not use it for, say, releasing a movie or a product; the verb for that is 发布 or 推出. Here's an example. 国务院表示，将出台一揽子政策稳定经济。(The State Council said it will roll out a package of policies to stabilize the economy.)
SAM: A useful contrast is with 制定, which means to formulate or draft. 制定 is the drafting stage, and 出台 is the moment it becomes public and takes effect. Another example: 政府刚出台了新的房地产措施。(The government has just rolled out new real-estate measures.)
ALEX: The second is a connective pair, 一边……一边……, yībiān yībiān, which means doing two things at the same time, or the same person or entity doing two things in parallel. It sits in the spoken and neutral register, and it is a good HSK 4 target because learners often confuse it with 同时.
SAM: What's the difference?
ALEX: 一边……一边…… links two actions by the same subject happening simultaneously, and usually reads as informal or conversational. 同时 is more abstract and formal, and it can connect two whole situations. In news writing, when you want to say a government is doing two contradictory things at once, 一边……一边…… gives it a slightly critical, vivid edge. For example: 地方政府一边缩减开支，一边努力稳住经济。(Local governments are cutting spending while also trying to keep the economy steady.)
SAM: And a second example with a very ordinary situation: 他一边吃早饭，一边看新闻。(He reads the news while eating breakfast.)
ALEX: So the pair for today is 出台, formal, for the moment a policy goes public, and 一边……一边……, conversational, for two simultaneous actions. Listen for both in the next segment.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
