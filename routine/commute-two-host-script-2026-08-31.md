## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Alex, you sent me something this morning that has nothing to do with markets or the AI buildout, and I want the full version on air.
ALEX: Adam Tooze's latest Chartbook is a literary essay — he's reading Ernst Jünger's 1939 novella "On the Marble Cliffs," and it's really about the psychology of the people running today's tech-power stack, even though it was written eighty-seven years ago.
SAM: Who was Jünger, for anyone who doesn't know the name?
ALEX: Decorated WWI hero — Germany's highest valor decoration, the Pour le Mérite — then a Weimar-era prophet of what Tooze calls "technological modernity," someone who genuinely glorified total mobilization and machine power. And then, unlike Heidegger or Carl Schmitt, he deliberately kept his distance from the actual Third Reich once it arrived.
SAM: So the novella is him reckoning with his own earlier worldview?
ALEX: That's Tooze's argument, and it's a sharp one. The book's setting literally fuses eras — Tooze's phrase is the "feudal-sci-fi mix" — powerful cars with powerful headlights, driven by men dressed in medieval aristocratic livery, in an invented realm called New Burgundy. He explicitly compares it to Roy Batty's "tears in rain" monologue in Blade Runner, decades before that even existed as a genre.
SAM: And there's a villain figure in this — is that meant to be Hitler?
ALEX: This is the part I want you to really sit with. Tooze says no — he calls the easy Hitler-or-Stalin reading "simplistic and implausible." The villain represents unleashed, wild violence as a type, not a specific dictator's project. The character Tooze actually zeroes in on is a different one — Braquemart, described as having "a cold, rootless intelligence with a penchant for utopias" who "insisted that ideas be armed, tooth and claw." Tooze reads Braquemart as Jünger's self-portrait — the man he used to be.
SAM: A technocrat who treats ideas as weapons and treats life as clockwork.
ALEX: That's exactly the phrase, and that's why I think this belongs in this show. Strip out the 1939 setting and that description — high-tech power fused with a self-styled aristocratic hierarchy, contempt for the people underneath it, ideas wielded like armaments — is precisely the lens people are already applying to today's AI and data-center oligarchy. Tech barons as neo-feudal lords running private power, land, and water empires.
SAM: Is Tooze making that connection explicitly, or is that you reading it in?
ALEX: He's writing about 1939 Germany, full stop — I'm the one drawing the line forward, and I think it holds up better than most of the AI-feudalism commentary that does try to make it explicitly, because Tooze's version comes with the psychological mechanism attached, not just the aesthetic.
SAM: What's the mechanism, exactly?
ALEX: Jünger's own answer, per Tooze, is almost startling given where he started — "the best weapon against nihilism is the change of the human heart." Not better institutions, not more technology, not counter-force — an actual internal reckoning. Coming from a man who once wrote "Storm of Steel" and genuinely believed in violent modernity, that's not a throwaway line.
SAM: There's a line in there Tooze quotes that I keep coming back to — "there is a profound difference between fully formed nihilism and unchecked anarchy."
ALEX: One wants slaves and captive armies — total control. The other wants to burn the whole structure down and return civilization to wild beasts. Jünger's point is those get treated as the same threat, and they're not, and mistaking one for the other is exactly how people misjudge who's actually dangerous in a moment like this.
SAM: It's a strange thing to find this useful for reading a 2026 tech landscape, but I actually think it is — it gives you a vocabulary for "which kind of dangerous" instead of just "dangerous."
ALEX: That's why it earned the full segment. Chartbook, issue 472, published Friday — worth the full read if any of that landed for you.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Two Mandarin words tied to China's new PMI data, and today's pairing is about holding two true things at once.
ALEX: First: 即使…也… — jíshǐ, yě. It means "even if X, Y still holds" — a more emphatic, almost extreme-case version of the basic 虽然…但是.
SAM: What's the actual difference in force between those two?
ALEX: 虽然…但是 is neutral, textbook-level — it just states a contrast. 即使…也 pushes the contrast to its edge — you reach for it to say "even in the best case, this would still be true," not just when two ordinary facts happen to differ. Example: 即使制造业数据有所改善，非制造业指数也停留在收缩区间。"Even though the manufacturing data improved somewhat, the non-manufacturing index still remains in contraction territory."
SAM: Second word — 低迷, dīmí.
ALEX: It means sluggish, or depressed, describing economic activity that's stuck below where it should be — 书面语, formal, the kind of word you'll see constantly in financial reporting, not something you'd say about your own mood.
SAM: What would you say instead in casual conversation?
ALEX: 不景气 works colloquially — "times are bad" — but 低迷 is more precise and more clinical; you'd use it specifically about a sector or an index, like 建筑业持续低迷, "the construction sector remains persistently sluggish." It pairs naturally with 需求, 消费, 楼市 — demand, consumption, the property market.
SAM: Both of those are doing real work in the next segment.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY

ALEX: Their argument is that Trump pausing visa applications worldwide directly contradicts his own stated position — "I'm fine with legal immigration," he's said repeatedly. SAM: Where do you push back on that?
ALEX: The whole argument assumes the administration's stated goal — curbing illegal overstays — is the real goal. ALEX: Here's the problem — stigma and usage aren't actually in tension the way she frames it. ALEX: Exactly — and until someone shows usage actually stalling because of the stigma, rather than just documenting that the stigma exists, the "bottleneck" framing is doing more work than the evidence supports.
