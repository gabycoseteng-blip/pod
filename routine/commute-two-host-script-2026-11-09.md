## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: For the Deep Dive we are going to slow down on the question underneath this whole week, which is not whether the AI trade is overvalued. It is what Tuesday's vote actually said. Our source is a piece in Foreign Policy from November 5 by Rishi Iyengar and Christina Lu, titled The Post-Midterms AI Reckoning, plus the NBC exit poll data it leans on.
ALEX: Their argument is that the election was, among other things, a referendum on data centers. Voters in Texas, Georgia and Ohio backed mostly Democratic candidates who promised to rein in AI development and slow the build-out, and the piece points to Texas as the clearest case, where so many data centers were built that they strained the state's grid and produced public outrage.
SAM: And the headline result there is a governor's race.
ALEX: According to Foreign Policy, Gina Hinojosa unseated Greg Abbott in Texas, and she had urged an immediate moratorium on data center development. Write-ups of the same night also list Amy Acton in Ohio and Keisha Lance Bottoms in Georgia as candidates who supported moratoria or greater accountability for developers. I would treat the candidate-by-candidate details with care, because the coverage is uneven, but the pattern is consistent across outlets.
SAM: What about the ballot measures?
ALEX: That is where the accounts disagree on the number, and the disagreement is instructive. One account says voters approved more than 40 measures to slow or ban data center growth. Another says voters rejected data center projects in 40 of 41 local ballot measures. Those are two ways of describing the same landslide, and neither is a rounding error. When local democracy says no 40 times out of 41, the pipeline is not facing a regulatory headwind, it is facing a veto at the county level.
SAM: And the national polling matches that.
ALEX: The NBC exit poll, as cited by Foreign Policy, found 57% of voters believe AI will do more harm than good to society, and 56% called AI a crucial or major factor in their vote. Separately, polling shows roughly 61% of Americans oppose a new data center in their own community. Against that, tech executives reportedly spent more than half a billion dollars backing Republican candidates, so the industry did not lose for lack of money.
SAM: So what is the argument about?
ALEX: Three contested questions. First, is this a cost-of-living vote in AI clothing? Electricity prices rose about 31% between 2020 and 2025, and the cleanest reading is that voters are punishing the visible bill, not the abstract technology. If that is right, the backlash is solvable with tariff design: make the data center pay, and the politics soften. That is the logic behind the Trump idea of a dividend to communities that host a facility.
SAM: And the second?
ALEX: Is it durable? A Democratic Congress cannot be seated until January 3, a presidential veto sits behind any bill, and Democrats are themselves split. Fetterman called the Sanders moratorium China First. The second reading is that Congress is mostly a megaphone, and the binding action is at the state and local level, where the margins are 40 to 1.
SAM: The third?
ALEX: Who captures the policy. The New Republic and The Nation have both argued Democrats have an opening on this issue, and the National Review's Corner warns that a populist crackdown could hit the engines of growth that are propping up stock prices. Reuters reported the new Congress is expected to apply more scrutiny to AI generally, including data centers, which raises volatility. Nobody serious thinks AI is going to be banned, so the live question is the terms of consent: who pays, who approves, and who gets the dividend.
SAM: Here is the second-order read for an investor.
ALEX: The thing being priced on Thursday was not a moratorium, it was the end of the assumption that permitting is a formality. For three years the model assumed the binding constraint was chips, then power. Now there is a third, consent, and it is the one that does not respond to capital. The companies that already own entitled, powered sites are worth more, and the ones that depend on a pipeline of new sites are worth less.
SAM: And for the listener who works in development?
ALEX: It changes the site-selection checklist. Community benefit agreements, a bring-your-own-generation story, and a credible answer to the residential rate question stop being nice-to-haves. The developer who can show the town its bills will not go up is the developer who gets a vote of 1 to 0 instead of 40 to 1.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
ALEX: The energy segment picks up a docket we have followed, because it is the one that decides how the consent politics we just covered get implemented. The new development is today's deadline. In October, FERC sent PJM's data center plan back to the drawing board. PJM had proposed in August that new data centers bring their own capacity and be barred from a key power auction.
SAM: Two months later, the regulators rejected the main pillars.
ALEX: They did, and the objection was substantially jurisdictional. FERC also rejected the curtailment plan, which would have cut power to data centers that did not bring their own supply, and approved a narrower piece, a registry to track data centers across PJM's footprint. Utility Dive's reading was that the question of who can impose conditions on a load sits with states, not the RTO. The registry compliance filing was due around today, November 9, and PJM was expected to refile a separate backstop procurement plan.
SAM: And the cost question is unresolved.
ALEX: Right. The market monitor estimates that data center load added $11.11 per megawatt-hour to the total wholesale cost of power in the first half of 2026. One analysis puts the unresolved allocation at $29.4 billion. In December 2025, FERC ordered PJM to create new transmission services for power plants serving co-located loads, which was the first sign the commission would let data centers buy a bypass of the capacity market.
SAM: So who wins from the rejection?
ALEX: States, and in particular the ones that already have large-load tariffs. Dominion's Virginia tariff demands collateral of roughly $1.5 million per megawatt, which is far more than Texas proposed. In Texas, SB 6 is being implemented by the Public Utility Commission with a batch process, called Batch Zero, for loads of 75 megawatts and above. A Foley summary of the proposed rules describes a $50,000 per megawatt nonrefundable fee and a 5-year recovery period, though I would verify that against the rule text.
ALEX: The bill, introduced March 25, would pause new AI data center construction immediately and keep the pause until laws are enacted ensuring they do not raise utility bills, harm communities or damage the environment. Ocasio-Cortez's line is that a hyperscale facility consumes the same amount of energy in one second as 100,000 households. Electricity rates rose about 31% between 2020 and 2025, and that is the political engine. The bill will not pass, but its test, that a new load must demonstrate it does not raise residential bills, is exactly what state commissions are writing into tariffs.
SAM: So the practitioner takeaway?
ALEX: Three things. One, interconnection is becoming a balance-sheet exercise. The developer with collateral, firm off-takers and bring-your-own-generation clears the queue, and everyone else is a placeholder. Two, the bring-your-own-capacity concept lost at FERC but wins in practice, because utilities will make it a condition of service. Three, a neocloud with 1.85 gigawatts targeted by year end and $38.6 billion of debt is a case study in what happens when interconnection risk meets credit risk.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Two Mandarin words today, both pulled from the China story that follows, and one of them is an advanced connective, which is the listener's HSK 4 weak spot. Alex, the first one.
ALEX: 难怪, nánguài. It means no wonder, and it introduces a result that becomes understandable once you know the cause. The structure is the result first, then the explanation, or the other way round with 因为 or 原来. So you say the effect with 难怪, and the reason follows. Register-wise it is neutral, comfortable in speech and in a news commentary.
SAM: Give us the example.
ALEX: 上游成本涨了这么多,难怪工厂的利润被压缩了。(Upstream costs have risen so much, so no wonder factories' margins have been squeezed.) And: 难怪消费者几乎没感觉到涨价,成本都被企业吞掉了。(No wonder consumers barely noticed the price rises, since businesses swallowed the costs.)
SAM: The near-synonym is 怪不得.
ALEX: Right, 怪不得, guàibude, means almost the same thing, and the difference is register. 怪不得 is more colloquial, the thing you would say to a friend, while 难怪 is slightly more at home in writing. A different word to keep apart is 当然, of course, which states something is obvious, whereas 难怪 specifically says something that looked puzzling now makes sense.
SAM: The second word is 传导, chuándǎo.
ALEX: It means to transmit or to conduct, in the physical sense of heat or electricity and in the economic sense of a mechanism passing an effect along. The collocation is 传导机制, transmission mechanism, or 价格传导, price pass-through. It is distinctly written register, finance-desk and policy language. The near-synonym is 传递, chuándì, which means to pass something along, including a message or an object, and it is more general and more colloquial. 传导 implies a channel with resistance, something that can be efficient or blocked.
SAM: An example?
ALEX: 上游成本很难传导到消费端。(Upstream costs are hard to pass through to the consumer end.) And: 货币政策的传导机制正在减弱。(The transmission mechanism of monetary policy is weakening.) Notice the abstract noun pairing, 传导机制, which is exactly the kind of formal collocation that shows up in central-bank statements.
SAM: And both of those words are doing real work in the China segment next.
