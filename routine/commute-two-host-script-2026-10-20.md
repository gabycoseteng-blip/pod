## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

SAM: Our deep dive today is a law story, and a follow-up to something we flagged earlier this month. On October 5th the Supreme Court heard argument in Suncor Energy versus Boulder County, case number 25-170. The sources here are the legal press and SCOTUSblog, not a single newsletter piece, so I will attribute as we go. Boulder and its county sued Exxon and 3 Suncor entities in Colorado state court back in 2018 for nuisance, trespass, unjust enrichment, civil conspiracy and deceptive trade practices, seeking damages and adaptation costs.
ALEX: The procedural history is why this one is strange. The companies kept trying to move the case to federal court and kept being sent back. Then the Colorado Supreme Court ruled 5 to 2 in May of last year that federal law does not preempt the claims. The companies petitioned, and the Court took 2 questions. First, does federal law, meaning the constitutional structure plus the Clean Air Act, preclude state-law claims for harm from interstate and international greenhouse-gas emissions? Second, and this was the surprise at argument, does the Court even have jurisdiction?
SAM: Because the Colorado ruling was not the end of the case. Suncor says it was a self-contained proceeding and therefore a final judgment. Boulder says it was an ordinary interlocutory appeal, and that Suncor has not suffered any concrete injury yet. Justice Thomas raised jurisdiction at the very start, Justice Jackson said review looked premature, and Justice Sotomayor pressed on it too. So you have 3 justices who are not on one side politically all asking whether the case is even properly here.
ALEX: And then there is the arithmetic. Justice Alito recused about a week before argument. That leaves 8 justices, and a 4 to 4 tie would simply affirm the Colorado ruling without a precedent, letting the case go to trial. Law Week Colorado and Just Security both flagged this. So the 2 realistic outcomes that look like anticlimaxes, a tie or a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction, both leave the big question open.
SAM: Now the merits, as reported by the court watchers. Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Kavanaugh sounded sympathetic to the industry. Kavanaugh said a wall of precedent makes it clear that interstate pollution claims are preempted, and warned that suits like this could bankrupt defendants. Roberts asked whether the suit is an end-run around the Court's own emissions precedents. The 3 liberal justices sounded more favorable to Boulder, and Justice Kagan questioned where the companies' structural theory finds support in text or precedent, saying the approach appears to be based on the old 1990s tobacco suits.
ALEX: The most quoted exchange is the one about limits. Boulder's lawyer, Kevin Russell, compared the case to a product-liability suit against a carmaker. Pressed by Thomas, he conceded the theory could in principle reach other sectors, gasoline and petrochemical makers and even dairy farmers because of methane. And that is the industry's whole argument in a sentence: if the theory has no limit, then state tort law becomes de facto national climate regulation, set by 50 juries. Boulder's answer is that its deception claims are distinct from emissions claims, that it is suing over what the companies told the public, not over the weather.
SAM: Justice Barrett reportedly observed that everyone agrees state tort law is a form of regulation, and Justice Gorsuch asked whether applying one state's law to worldwide conduct is anomalous. Put those 2 together and you can see the shape of a conservative-friendly federalism argument that does not need the Clean Air Act at all. The Trump administration, per CPR, urged the Court to end the case.
ALEX: Why it matters for this audience. First, scale. The ruling is described as affecting more than 2 dozen similar suits by cities, counties and states, so the liability tail for oil majors is a valuation question, not a legal curiosity. Second, the deception framing. Advocates on both sides note that a loss for Boulder could also shield other industries from state deception claims, social media and pharma being the ones people name. Third, and this is my own inference, not something in the reporting, the same logic is a possible template for suits aimed at gas-fired power built to serve data centers. If a court says a locality can sue for adaptation costs, every large emitter's counsel starts modelling it.
SAM: So what do we actually expect? The decision is not due until roughly June 2027. My read is that the jurisdiction fight is the likeliest off-ramp, because it lets a divided Court avoid the merits.
ALEX: I would put it slightly differently. With Alito out, the 4 to 4 tie is the outcome that nobody would plan for and everybody should price. It would leave Boulder's case alive in a Colorado court, with no national ruling, and the next petition from a different state would arrive with 9 justices. Either way, the question we started this segment with is not being answered this term.
SAM: And that is the pattern to take away. A case everybody treats as a climate case turns out to be decided, if it is decided at all, by procedure and by who is sitting. Anyone modelling the liability tail should be modelling the procedure.
ALEX: Same instinct as Macron and Nuñez, really. The question of who gets to say it is over is rarely answered by the person asking.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

ALEX: Two Mandarin words today, both from the China data. First, 乃至, nǎizhì.
SAM: It means "and even," and it is a written-register connective that stretches a list up to its far end. Think of it as 甚至 in a suit. 甚至 is the spoken intensifier you hear with 都 or 也, while 乃至 sits in reports and editorials and links nouns or numbers on a scale.
ALEX: An example: 房地产投资大幅下滑，乃至拖累了整体投资。(Property investment fell sharply, even dragging down total investment.)
SAM: The collocation to remember is a list that climbs: 企业，乃至整个行业, firms, and even the whole industry. It rarely starts a sentence in casual speech. If you hear it in conversation, the speaker is deliberately sounding formal.
ALEX: Second, 好在, hǎozài, "fortunately," and this one is spoken-register. It opens the clause that rescues a bad situation.
SAM: The near-synonym is 幸亏, which stresses a narrow escape and pairs with 才 or 不然 for what would have happened otherwise. 好在 is milder. It just points at the saving factor without implying disaster.
ALEX: Here is one: 内需疲软，好在出口依然强劲。(Domestic demand is soft, but fortunately exports remain strong.)
SAM: And a second, to show the rhythm: 价格上涨了，好在工资也涨了。(Prices went up, but fortunately wages also went up.)
ALEX: Notice the structure. First the problem, then 好在, then the rescue. It is a very natural way to deliver bad-news-with-a-caveat, which is most of the finance desk's work. 乃至 and 好在, a formal connective and a colloquial one.
