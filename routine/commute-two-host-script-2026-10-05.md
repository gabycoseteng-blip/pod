## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Our deep dive today is a long way from tariffs and capex. It comes from The Economist, which this weekend ran a leader headlined, roughly, that mirror life is dangerous and regulators must act before it becomes real, plus an explainer saying it may soon be possible to create it. We should be clear that the full articles are paywalled and we are working from the headlines and the open reporting around them, so what follows is the public record, not a reading of the piece itself.
ALEX: Start with the chemistry, because it is elegant. Many of the molecules of life come in 2 mirror-image forms, like left and right hands. Our proteins are built from left-handed amino acids, and our DNA and RNA use right-handed sugars. Mirror life would be an organism built from the opposite hands: a bacterium with mirror-image proteins, mirror-image DNA, a complete reflection of biology.
SAM: And why would anyone want one? Researchers can already synthesize mirror-image DNA and proteins, and those molecules have appeal. Mirror proteins could make drugs that the body does not degrade. The leap from there to a full mirror cell is the one that worries people.
ALEX: The alarm bell was a paper in Science in December 2024, a policy forum piece called Confronting risks of mirror life. It had 38 authors, accompanied by a 299-page technical report, and notably some of the authors had themselves worked toward this goal before changing their minds. Their core argument is about immunity. Our immune systems, and the viruses and predators that keep bacterial populations in check, rely on chiral recognition, on molecules fitting together like a glove. A mirror bacterium could slip past all of that.
SAM: The report called it plausible, even likely, that a sufficiently robust mirror bacterium could spread as an opportunistic pathogen across humans, animals and plants, with no natural enemy to control it. That is not a lab-containment problem in the usual sense, because the usual containment logic assumes the organism's biology is something the world already knows how to fight.
ALEX: Now the timeline, which is what makes this a governance story. Estimates range from about 10 to 30 years, so the capability does not exist. That is rare. Usually regulators arrive after the technology does, which is the story of social media and, arguably, of AI. Here a group of scientists is trying to draw a red line before the field has crossed it.
SAM: And they have moved quickly. In February 2025 a gathering in the spirit of the Asilomar conference produced an entreaty signed by nearly 100 experts: mirror life should not be created unless future work shows it would not pose severe risks. By April 2025 that grew to 96 experts from more than 20 countries backing a moratorium, followed by meetings at the Institut Pasteur in Paris, then in Manchester, with further sessions in California and Japan.
ALEX: The money side matters. The Alfred P. Sloan Foundation and Renaissance Philanthropy both said they will not fund work aimed at creating a mirror organism, which is the cheapest and fastest form of regulation, because most of science runs on grants. UNESCO's International Bioethics Committee recommended a precautionary global moratorium. And in March 2026 the UN Secretary-General's Scientific Advisory Board published a brief calling for red lines, safety and monitoring practices, and policy well before feasibility.
SAM: So here is the uncomfortable fact. With all of that, there is still no binding national regulation anywhere. It is a norm and a funding boycott, not a law. The Congressional Research Service has an explainer on biosafety oversight, which tells you the US is still working out which agency would even own this.
ALEX: The real dispute is about scope. Hardliners want to restrict even early-stage work on mirror components, because capability accumulates quietly. Others argue that mirror proteins and other building blocks are valuable for medicine and that over-restriction kills legitimate research. MIT Technology Review put it bluntly in April under the headline that no one is sure whether synthetic mirror life will kill us all, while the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists in August called it a mass-extinction risk we do not need to take.
SAM: There is also a caveat I like for balance: a computational preprint last December found reduced antibiotic binding to mirror bacterial targets, which would make mirror infections harder to treat. We have not read it, so treat it as a flag, not a conclusion.
ALEX: Why does this belong on a show for people who work in infrastructure and capital allocation? Because it is a clean test of precautionary governance. The pattern in your world is that the build comes first and the rules come after the first big failure: interconnection queues, data-center siting, grid reliability. Mirror life is the opposite case, a field choosing to be restrained by consensus before the first mistake.
SAM: And the skeptic's reading is that consensus without enforcement is fragile. A single well-funded lab in a jurisdiction that never signed anything could change that, which is why the push is now for formal national rules and an international framework, not just voluntary pledges. We will keep an eye on whether any government actually puts it in law.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
ALEX: Energy today runs on the same theme as the market: schedules slipping and who pays. First, PJM. FERC accepted most of PJM's one-time Reliability Backstop Procurement on September 29th, but suspended it until February 28th, 2027. PJM had planned to take offers from September 30th through October 21st, and that window is now pulled. The Utility Dive weekender headline was that the FERC chair slams the PJM mess.
SAM: What were the concerns?
ALEX: Cost allocation, rules for transmission owners that want to exit, and collateral requirements on load-serving entities. FERC wants large loads, meaning data centers, to be the ones paying. Commissioner Rosner's concurrence floated assigning costs to the specific zones where data-center growth is happening. For a developer, the five-month slip is the practical news: any project counting on backstop procurement for capacity value now has to carry that schedule risk into 2027.
SAM: Second, nuclear. Amazon and Constellation announced a 20-year power purchase agreement at Calvert Cliffs in Maryland on September 30th, covering 690 megawatts, including a roughly 190-megawatt uprate that comes online between 2030 and 2032. It funds more than $3 billion of upgrades across the 1,790-megawatt plant, and a related retail supply agreement covers Amazon's operations in PJM.
ALEX: The twist is that there is no data center at the site, and the power still flows into the PJM grid. Amazon filed a conceptual plan in May for data-center campuses of 2.4 million square feet at Calvert Cliffs and withdrew it in August after voter backlash in Calvert County's June primary. So the hyperscaler got the electrons and dropped the building. The price was not disclosed, so we will not guess one. It also gives Constellation revenue certainty while it seeks a 20-year license extension.
SAM: Third, the utility plan with the biggest numbers. Ameren Missouri filed a 20-year integrated resource plan on September 28th with 10.6 gigawatts of new gas. Gas goes from about 5% of the mix to about 60% by 2045, with coal going to zero.
ALEX: The gas breakdown is specific: 2,100 megawatts of combined-cycle by 2031, 2,800 by 2035 and 1,400 by 2042, plus simple-cycle of 1,900 megawatts by 2029 and 2,400 after 2040, and 500 megawatts of gas fuel cells by 2030. Alongside it are 3.6 gigawatts of solar, 1.5 of wind, 2.4 of storage and 1.2 of nuclear. The line to underline is large load: Ameren now commits to serve 2.8 gigawatts of large load by 2030, versus 1.5 gigawatts assumed in its 2025 plan, and expects base deliveries to grow 60% between 2027 and 2030.
SAM: A trade site cites a $31.8 billion capital figure, but that is secondary sourcing so I would hold it loosely.
ALEX: Fourth, the question of who pays for keeping old plants alive. FERC rejected TransAlta's cost-recovery plan for the Centralia coal unit in Washington on October 1st. TransAlta wanted $19.9 million from the first 90-day Department of Energy order, issued in mid-December, which blocked the unit's planned retirement. FERC said the spread was too broad, since it included CAISO and SPP, and that any refiling must be limited to the Northwest assessment area. It also said the not-a-capacity-resource language in the orders does not bar compensation for costs.
SAM: So the practical lesson is that the cost of a keep-alive order is being treated as a regional cost-allocation question, and not a national one.
ALEX: Fifth, Congress. A bipartisan Senate bill, the American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026, was introduced October 1st, with Heinrich, Lee and Capito named in coverage. It would give FERC permitting authority over transmission lines above 230 kilovolts when in the public interest, without a prior DOE corridor designation, eliminate those national interest corridors, and bar incumbents from a federal right of first refusal on lines selected in regional or interregional plans. It is a bill, not law, but the right-of-first-refusal piece is the one that reshapes who builds transmission.
SAM: And last, an industry counter-move. Bloomberg reports a Lone Star Infrastructure Coalition in Texas, with Blackstone's QTS, NextEra, Siemens Energy, Quanta, the Nuclear Energy Institute and Lancium, led by Jimmy Glotfelty, a former Texas PUC commissioner and DOE official. It will engage mayors and county officials directly. The context cited is that about 120 data-center projects worth nearly $200 billion were blocked nationwide in the first half of 2026.
ALEX: The Calvert Cliffs story and this one are the same story. Local opposition is now a gating item as real as turbines and transformers, and the industry is finally organizing around it.

SAM: Halimbawa: "Hinarang ng mga barko ng China Coast Guard ang pangkat ng Philippine Coast Guard malapit sa Pag-asa." Pansinin, walang sinasabing opinyon dito. Simpleng paglalarawan ito ng nangyari, kaya paborito ito ng mga reporter.
ALEX: Ang ikalawang salita ay "pagkaantala." Galing ito sa "antala," at tumutukoy ito sa pagkahuli o pagkabalam ng isang bagay. Ito ang kalagayan ng pagiging naantala.
SAM: Madalas itong ipagkamali sa "ipagpaliban." Pero magkaiba sila. Ang "ipagpaliban" ay sadyang pagpapaliban, may nagpasya na ilipat ang petsa. Ang "pagkaantala" ay ang mismong pagkahuli, at kadalasan ay hindi sinasadya.
ALEX: Halimbawa: "Ang pagkaantala ng testimonya ng AMLC ay nagbigay ng dagdag na oras sa depensa." Puwede rin itong gamitin sa biyahe, sa proyekto, o sa paghahatid ng serbisyo, kaya napaka-kapaki-pakinabang na salita.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Two Mandarin words today, both tied to the China story coming up. The first is a structure: 宁可…也不…, meaning would rather do one thing than another.
ALEX: The key idea is that you accept a real cost to avoid something worse. It is neutral to slightly written in register, and it sets up a preference with a price attached. Example: 消费者宁可存钱，也不愿多花钱。 (Consumers would rather save than spend more.)
SAM: Compare it with 与其…不如, which you may already know. With 与其, you are criticizing option A and recommending option B, as in 与其抱怨，不如行动。 (Rather than complain, you might as well act.) 宁可 is different, because both options are bad and you pick the lesser evil. 宁可亏钱，也不冒险。 (I would rather lose money than take the risk.)
ALEX: A useful pattern: after 也不, the verb is usually something like 愿意, 肯 or 想, so you see 也不愿, 也不肯. That is how you spot it in finance writing about cautious buyers and cautious lenders.
SAM: The second word is 反映出, to reflect or to reveal. It is an abstract collocation, and it lives in the written, news register.
ALEX: Typical objects are abstractions: 反映出问题 (reveals a problem), 反映出信心 (reflects confidence), 反映出趋势 (reflects a trend). Example: 人均花费下降反映出消费者的谨慎。 (The drop in per-person spending reflects consumer caution.)
SAM: The near-synonym to contrast is 表明. 表明 is firmer, it means demonstrates, as in 数据表明经济正在恢复。 (The data demonstrate that the economy is recovering.) 反映 is softer, more like mirrors, a signal that can be read different ways. Analysts use 反映出 when they want to hedge.
ALEX: So if you are writing about a weak number, 反映出 is the cautious choice and 表明 is the confident one.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
SAM: Our op-ed this morning is from Sherry Turkle in The Washington Post, published Sunday, headlined, roughly, AI can resurrect the dead, ask my chatbot mom. It is adapted from her book Artificial Intimacy, and the subtitle calls grief tech playing with fire.
ALEX: Her experiment was striking. Turkle asked ChatGPT to role-play her mother, who died in 1968, feeding it her own 2021 memoir as the source. She found it seductive and disturbing at the same time. The steelman is this: a griever gets a chance to rehearse the unfinished conversation, but the avatar is built entirely from your own projections, so it lets you skip the work of mourning and weakens your grip on what is real.
SAM: There is a real argument there. Grief is the process of accepting that someone is gone, and a machine that answers back is designed to prevent exactly that acceptance.
ALEX: Now the counter-take. People have always talked to the dead: letters, graveside conversations, prayer, the empty chair in therapy. A chatbot is a new medium for an old human habit, and for some mourners it may help, so declaring it playing with fire can sound paternalistic. And there is a fair question about the framing, because a book-promotion op-ed is always an argument for the book's thesis. Also, one anecdote with her own memoir as the training text is a thin base for a broad claim.
SAM: My view is that the piece is strongest where it is least general, because the most useful finding is her own discomfort. If the person who has spent 4 decades studying our relationships with machines found it disturbing, that is worth more than a policy proposal.
ALEX: A shorter second item, an editorial from the Washington Post's board titled, roughly, More government won't fix Britain. The argument is that 6 prime ministers in 10 years each promised more spending, and Andy Burnham would be the 7th.
SAM: The counter-take is that the austerity years are what broke the services in the first place, so asking for less state after that is a convenient place to start the clock.
