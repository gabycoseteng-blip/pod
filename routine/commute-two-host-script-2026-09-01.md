## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Alex, you sent me something this morning that's got nothing to do with markets or the AI buildout, and I want it on air in full.
ALEX: It's an interview from Sublime's Humanmaxxing series — Alex Dobrenko talking to Adam Mastroianni, who writes the Substack Experimental History and has a book coming, "The Strangest Idea in the World." Published Friday.
SAM: What's the actual argument?
ALEX: That LLMs have digested essentially the whole internet, so they can cheaply produce anything that's derivable from consensus text — and that fact forces writers and thinkers toward genuine idiosyncrasy instead of synthesis, because synthesis is now free. His claim is that good writing was never retrieval, it's generative — you don't know the ending when you start, his phrase is "building a bridge over a chasm, one plank at a time."
SAM: So the model can only ever produce the bridge someone's already built.
ALEX: That's the crux. He draws a distinction between "thick" work and "thin" work — human work has thickness, it rewards repeated attention, you notice more the longer you sit with it. AI output is thin — looks fine on first pass, and the more closely you look, the less is actually there.
SAM: Is he anxious about that, given what it implies for a lot of white-collar writing?
ALEX: Genuinely not — he frames it as a forcing function. If synthesis is commoditized, then the only work worth paying for is distinctive, high-taste work, and that's a real filter, not a tragedy. There's one more piece I think lands hardest for this audience specifically — he says only humans can put something at stake. Reputation, real risk, an actual stated memory. A machine can't lie about having been 13 once, because it never was.
SAM: That's a strange, specific way to make the point, and it's sticking with me.
ALEX: It should — because it reframes the whole AI-labor-displacement debate we usually approach from the capex and power side. This is the demand-side version: attention and taste are the moat once generation itself is free, not fluency.
SAM: Does he offer any test for telling thick work from thin work in practice, or is it purely a feeling you get?
ALEX: He gives a rough one — thin work degrades the more attention you put into it, because there was never more than the surface pass to begin with. Thick work rewards a second and third look with something new each time, because a real person made real, specific choices you can keep discovering. His test for writing specifically: could you have predicted every sentence from the one before it? If yes, you were watching synthesis, not thought.
SAM: That's actually a usable heuristic for reading anything right now, not just prose.
ALEX: Which is exactly why it earned the full segment. Worth the full read — Humanmaxxing, on Substack, this past Friday.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: Staying with things that sound abstract until you look at the paperwork — PJM just dropped an Oklo nuclear project from its interconnection queue.
ALEX: This is Cycle 01, 750 megawatts total — 150 megawatts of advanced nuclear, 300 of fuel cells, 300 of gas, all behind one point of interconnection, queued back in April. PJM cited six deficiency categories: transformer data, generator data, project capability, point-of-interconnection detail, site control, short-circuit data.
SAM: 6 categories is a lot to fail at once — which of those is actually the killer?
ALEX: Voltage ride-through — Oklo never demonstrated it, and that's the one PJM won't wave through on a promise. Oklo filed an emergency FERC complaint on the 28th, docket EL26-101, demanding reinstatement at its original queue position and claiming it can cure the gaps inside PJM's 10-day window.
SAM: What does this tell you about where the real bottleneck sits right now?
ALEX: That for gigawatt-scale queue positions, the binding constraint has shifted from capital or siting to grid-code compliance paperwork. PJM's using technical screening to prune queue congestion, and any project without ride-through data already in hand is exposed to exactly the same drop Oklo just took.
SAM: Is this specific to novel mixed-tech projects, or could an established gas or solar developer get caught the same way?
ALEX: Any technology can fail a ride-through test, but mixed-tech, single-POI projects like Oklo's are structurally more exposed, because you're proving grid behavior for three different generation types behaving as one interconnection customer. That's a harder modeling problem than a single-technology plant, and it's exactly the kind of project advanced-nuclear and hybrid developers are betting the next capex cycle on.
SAM: What's the actual timeline if the FERC complaint doesn't go Oklo's way?
ALEX: Losing the original queue position means re-entering behind everyone who's applied since April — in PJM's current backlog, that's realistically a multi-year setback, not a paperwork delay. That's the real stake behind an emergency complaint that sounds, on its face, like a small procedural dispute.
SAM: Second story's almost the opposite move — someone just bought their way past the queue entirely.
ALEX: Hull Street Energy closed on 1,263 megawatts of gas peakers — the GridFlex portfolio, Lee County Illinois and Tait Ohio — from Rockland Capital on the 26th. That's thirteen days after FERC rejected PJM's own Independent Market Monitor, which had tried to condition approval on a promise the plants wouldn't be pointed at data centers.
SAM: So FERC took that lever off the table.
ALEX: Completely — which means merger conditions are no longer a tool regulators can use to ring-fence existing capacity from hyperscale offtake. Buying already-interconnected gas is now, full stop, the fastest legal path to committed megawatts, and this deal is the proof of concept.
SAM: Last one's about how transmission gets sized when a data center might show up later.
ALEX: Maine's PUC picked Clearway for 800 megawatts of onshore wind on the 25th — Maine's contracted share is about 600 of that — paired with an Avangrid transmission line, 111 miles, rated at 1,200 megawatts to Haynesville. That's roughly 50% oversized relative to the actual generation behind it.
SAM: Who's paying for the extra headroom nobody's using yet?
ALEX: Cost-shared across Maine, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Vermont, and Rhode Island — Maine's on the hook for under 11% of it, and it still needs separate FERC transmission approval. That "spare" roughly 400 megawatts is exactly the kind of pre-built transfer capacity that gets contested later when a large load wants to tap it — this is the template large-load developers elsewhere are already fighting utilities over.
SAM: So building oversized on purpose is now a deliberate strategy, not an engineering accident.
ALEX: It is, and it's a rational one for the states paying the small share — Maine gets optionality on future load growth for under 11% of the bill, while the multi-state cost allocation absorbs the risk that the extra capacity never gets used. The fight comes later, when a hyperscaler shows up wanting that spare 400 megawatts and every ratepayer who helped fund it wants a cut of the revenue.

## SEGMENT FIVE — TAGALOG VOCAB
SAM: Dalawang salita bago tayo pumunta sa Pilipinas, dahil parehong lalabas agad sa balita.
ALEX: Una, "pagbagsak." Ibig sabihin, ang biglaang o malaking pagbaba ng halaga o antas ng isang bagay — presyo, ranggo, kalagayan.
SAM: Paano ito naiiba sa simpleng "pagbaba," na mas neutral?
ALEX: Ang "pagbaba" ay puwedeng banayad at unti-unti — pagbaba ng temperatura, halimbawa. Ang "pagbagsak" ay may bigat at kadalasang biglaan — may implikasyon ito ng pagkabigo o krisis, hindi lang pangkaraniwang pagbabago — kaya ito mismo ang salitang gagamitin natin pagdating sa piso.
SAM: Ang pangalawa, "pagmamadali" — hindi ba simpleng "bilis" lang ito?
ALEX: Hindi rin — ang "bilis" ay neutral, walang negatibong kahulugan. Ang "pagmamadali" ay may kasamang implikasyon na kulang sa ingat o kulang sa lubos na pagsusuri, dahil sa pagnanais na matapos agad ang isang bagay.
SAM: Kaya kapag sinabi nating "pinagmadali" ang isang desisyon, may bahid tayo ng pagdududa.
ALEX: Eksakto — at kadalasan, kapag pinagmamadali ang isang bagay, may sinasakripisyo, maging ito man ay maingat na pagsusuri o tamang proseso. Iyon ang tensyon na dapat nating hanapin kapag naririnig natin ang salitang ito sa balita.
SAM: Kaya parehong salita ito, "pagbagsak" at "pagmamadali," ang gagamitin natin ngayon sa pagtalakay sa piso at sa badyet, kaya makikita ninyo agad kung paano sila umaangkop sa mismong balita.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Two Mandarin words tied to the sanctions volley ahead of Xi's Washington trip, and today's pairing is about the vocabulary of a negotiation, not a scoreboard.
ALEX: First: 筹码 — chóumǎ. Literally a gambling chip, but in policy and business talk it means leverage — something you deliberately stack up specifically to spend at the table later.
SAM: How's that different from just saying "优势," advantage?
ALEX: 优势 is a general edge — being better at something, full stop. 筹码 is accumulated on purpose, and crucially it's spendable — you build it up specifically to trade away for something else. It's the vocabulary of a negotiation, not a scoreboard. Here's the sentence: 双方都想在峰会前多攒一些筹码。"Both sides want to stack up more leverage before the summit."
SAM: Second — 既是…也是…, jìshì, yěshì.
ALEX: "Is both X and Y" — you use it to say one single thing genuinely serves two functions at once, rather than picking one description.
SAM: And how's that different from the more casual "又…又…"?
ALEX: 又…又… is casual, listing two simultaneous qualities equally, usually about a personal state — tired and hungry, say. 既是…也是… is more formal and analytical — it's the construction for saying a single policy or action carries two distinct identities at once. Ours: 这波制裁既是施压手段，也是峰会前的筹码。"This round of sanctions is both a pressure tactic and a bargaining chip ahead of the summit."

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
SAM: 2 op-eds worth arguing with. First, Gautam Mukunda in Bloomberg Opinion, responding to Bill Gates.
ALEX: Gates' essay from last week argued AI is "either the greatest equalizer ever invented or the worst source of injustice," and proposed taxing AI and robot labor since slowing development globally isn't realistic. Mukunda's response is that leaving safety choices to the AI labs themselves is naive — commercial and geopolitical competition will always override voluntary caution, full stop.
SAM: Where do you push back on Mukunda?
ALEX: Government regulation moves far slower than the model-capability curve does, and any regulatory regime risks capture by whichever lab lobbies hardest — OpenAI and Anthropic are already shaping open-weight policy in their own favor. So Mukunda's fix risks entrenching the incumbents he's worried about rather than protecting anyone from them.
SAM: Is there a version of this that avoids both problems?
ALEX: Probably not a clean one — but "regulate the labs" and "tax the labor displacement" that Gates actually proposed are solving two different problems, and Mukunda's piece conflates them. A capability-focused regulator and a labor-market tax are different instruments on different timelines, and treating them as one debate is part of why neither side's proposal quite lands.
SAM: Second piece — Megan McArdle in the Washington Post, on who actually counts as "elite."
ALEX: Her point is that populist rhetoric, left and right, invokes "the elite" so incoherently that no one agrees whether it means a university president, a streamer with millions of followers, or a billionaire — and that incoherence itself is corroding institutional trust, because any authority can be branded illegitimate under a label nobody's actually defined.
SAM: I don't think that's incoherence, though — isn't that just three genuinely different groups?
ALEX: That's exactly the counter — economic, cultural, and political elites are distinct groups with frequently conflicting interests, and treating "elite" as one monolithic class is what actually erases the real differences in power. McArdle diagnoses the symptom, incoherent rhetoric, but the disease is closer to people conflating three separate hierarchies on purpose, because it's rhetorically convenient to do so.
SAM: Give me a concrete case where that conflation actually does real damage.
ALEX: Take a tech executive attacked as "elite" for supporting an immigration policy — the attack borrows moral weight from economic-elite resentment while the actual object is a cultural or political disagreement. The word does the work of an argument nobody actually has to make, and that's the more precise version of McArdle's complaint than "nobody agrees what it means."
