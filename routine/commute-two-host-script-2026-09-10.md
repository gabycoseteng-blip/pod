## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

SAM: Deep dive today's a genuine left turn — out of markets and energy, into a math scandal that's actually a preview of something bigger.

ALEX: OpenAI announced that an internal, not-yet-public model ran roughly 10,000 coordinating AI agents for 88 hours and produced a 165-page proof, plus a machine-checked formal verification in a system called Lean 4, of a finite-time singularity in the 3D Navier-Stokes equations — a partial resolution of one of the six remaining Clay Millennium Prize problems.

SAM: Is that as big a deal as it sounds, or is there a catch?

ALEX: Two catches, actually. First, OpenAI isn't claiming the $1 million Clay Prize itself — the Institute requires roughly two years of publication and community vetting before it'll even consider one, so this is an announcement awaiting adjudication, not a settled result. Second, and this is where it gets genuinely ugly — an NYU mathematician named Tristan Buckmaster came out publicly accusing OpenAI of essentially stealing his work to get there.

SAM: Walk me through that accusation.

ALEX: Buckmaster and a co-author, Levent Alpöge, who works at Anthropic, had spent about a year working the adjacent Euler-equations problem — using AI coding tools, including OpenAI's own Codex product, along the way. Buckmaster's claim is that OpenAI's math push was triggered by a viral rumor that Anthropic had quietly solved two Millennium problems, and that OpenAI's model may have effectively benefited from his and Alpöge's private, unpublished research, because that research lived inside OpenAI's own infrastructure the whole time.

SAM: Does OpenAI dispute that?

ALEX: They deny any IP misappropriation, but VentureBeat's reporting is blunt about it — OpenAI itself says it "can't rule out" that the model benefited from that private data. And there's a second allegation on top: Buckmaster says OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck twice pushed to drop Alpöge from co-authorship specifically because he's at Anthropic, allegedly telling Buckmaster, quote, "Why would you ruin your career?"

SAM: What actually connects the two proofs, technically?

ALEX: Both OpenAI's result and Buckmaster and Alpöge's Euler proof build on the same underlying approach, from mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa — so a chunk of this fight is really about who gets credit for the last mile on a shared foundation, not who originated the idea from nothing.

SAM: Terence Tao weighed in on this too, right?

ALEX: He did, and his warning is the part that outlasts this specific dispute — he says AI-lab flag-planting races on trophy problems will make mathematicians afraid to discuss promising in-progress ideas openly, on blogs, in preprints, at seminars, which is exactly the open-collaboration norm the field has run on for centuries. He also criticized labs for publishing polished final answers while withholding the failed attempts and reasoning process mathematicians normally value just as much as the result.

SAM: Why does this matter to someone who isn't a research mathematician?

ALEX: Because it's the first real test case of frontier labs turning their compute arms race on scientific credit itself, not benchmarks or market share — and it's a genuine governance preview for anyone using a lab's coding agent on their own unpublished, proprietary work. If a lab's own product can effectively pull a user's private R&D into how that same lab allocates its next research push — deniably, without any single person reading the files — that's a data-gravity risk that has nothing to do with whether the underlying formal proof is actually sound.

SAM: And is the proof itself trustworthy, separate from all the credit drama?

ALEX: The Lean formal verification means the logic is machine-checked and locally valid — that part isn't really in dispute. But formal verification proves the argument holds together, it says nothing about priority, originality, or whether the result would exist at all without the human research it's quietly built on top of. That distinction is going to come up every single time a lab claims a scientific first from here on.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

SAM: Two Mandarin words before China — one's a formal connective you'll hear constantly in regulatory language, the other's the exact financial term behind today's story.

ALEX: First: 鉴于 — jiànyú. It means "in view of" or "given that" — a formal connective you use to introduce the reasoning or circumstance behind an official decision. It's heavily 书面语, written and formal register — you'd see it opening a government notice or a regulatory filing far more than in casual conversation.

SAM: How does it differ from just saying 因为, "because"?

ALEX: 因为 is neutral, works anywhere, spoken or written. 鉴于 specifically signals "this is the official, considered reason behind a formal action" — it's the word a regulator reaches for right before announcing a decision, which is exactly the register today's story lives in.

SAM: Second word — and this one's the actual mechanism at the center of today's China story.

ALEX: 违约 — wéiyuē. It means "to default" or "breach of contract" — literally "违," to violate, plus "约," an agreement. It's the standard financial and legal term for what happens when a borrower fails to meet a debt obligation.

SAM: Is that different from just saying someone "didn't pay"?

ALEX: Yes — 违约 is the formal, contractual framing. It's not just "didn't pay," it's "broke the terms of a binding agreement," which carries legal weight and triggers specific consequences under Chinese corporate law. It's the term you'll hear in every serious conversation about China's property-debt crisis.

SAM: Put them together the way you'd actually hear this on a finance desk?

ALEX: 鉴于监管收紧，越来越多的房企可能被迫违约。 ("Given the tightening regulatory environment, more and more property developers may be forced into default.") That's essentially the sentence behind today's China story, just with the actual numbers attached.

## SEGMENT EIGHT — CHINA

ALEX: 鉴于监管收紧，越来越多的房企可能被迫违约。 Given the tightening regulatory environment, more and more property developers may be forced into default. SAM: So regulators are tightening the exact same week their own policy is squeezing developer cash flow.

ALEX: That's the two-speed contradiction here — Beijing's tightening the regulatory spigot on debt workouts at the precise moment its own housing reforms are further straining developer and local-government finances. SAM: What does that say about Beijing's actual priorities here?

ALEX: It reads like regulators prioritizing control over which "discredited" firms get market access, over actually completing the deleveraging cycle the country needs.
