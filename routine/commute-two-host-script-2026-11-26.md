## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Our deep dive today comes from The Economist, which landed in the inbox on Tuesday night under a headline we couldn't skip: the rich world's sexual recession is getting worse. The piece's argument, from the paper's own summary, is that across rich countries people are having their first sexual experiences later in life and having sex less often, that fewer people are hooking up and fewer are moving in together, and that exhaustion is just one of the culprits.
ALEX: And the news hook is fresh data. This week The Lancet published the first papers from Natsal-4, Britain's once-a-decade national survey of sexual attitudes and lifestyles. It covers about 6,700 people aged 16 to 59 in England, Scotland and Wales, interviewed between September 2022 and January 2024. The headline number: for Britons aged 16 to 44, median frequency was 4 times a month in 1990, when the first Natsal ran. In the new survey it's 2.
SAM: Halved in a generation. And it isn't only the young. Among sexually active people aged 35 to 44, the median fell from 3 a month to 2. The young-adult number is the one getting attention, though: among 16 to 24 year olds, 38% of women and 28% of men say they've never had sex.
ALEX: The American data says the same thing from a different survey. The General Social Survey has the share of adults aged 18 to 64 having sex weekly falling from 55% in 1990 to 37% in 2024. Among 18 to 29 year olds, the share reporting no sex in the past year doubled between 2010 and 2024, from 12% to 24%. And marriage, which used to be the buffer, isn't anymore: weekly sex among married people fell from 59% in the 1996 to 2008 period to 49% in 2010 to 2024.
SAM: Japan is the extreme case. A review of 43 surveys from 1974 to 2024 finds that in online surveys from the 2020s, roughly half of adults aged 20 to 49 are sexually inactive.
ALEX: So what's driving it? This is where I want to be careful, because the honest answer is nobody knows, and the Natsal authors themselves say they couldn't establish why. The candidates line up like suspects. Smartphones and the attention economy. Pornography, and in Natsal-4, 81% of men and 37% of women aged 16 to 59 report watching it. Long hours and fatigue, which is The Economist's exhaustion point. Economic insecurity: if you can't afford your own flat, you live with your parents or with flatmates, and that's not a setting that encourages a sex life. People coupling later or not at all. Even the young drinking less, which sounds like a joke and isn't, because alcohol has always done a lot of the social lubrication.
SAM: And the coupling piece is measurable. The Economist has separately reported that living alone rose in 26 of 30 OECD countries between 2010 and 2022. Fewer couples, more single households.
ALEX: Now the contested part, and it's serious. The data is self-reported, the surveys don't define sex consistently, and response rates have collapsed. Natsal-4's response rate was about 24%. The 2024 General Social Survey hit a record-low response rate of 44.6%, after method changes since 2021. A paper this year in the journal Sexually Transmitted Infections found evidence of changing bias between Natsal-3 and Natsal-4. So part of the measured decline could be about who now answers surveys, and the people most likely to answer may be systematically different from the people who don't.
SAM: Then again, when 3 different survey systems in 3 different countries, with different methods, all point the same way, you can discount the size without dismissing the direction.
ALEX: That's where I land. The direction is robust; the magnitude is uncertain. And one caution from the researchers themselves: Kaye Wellings of the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine warns against drawing a straight line from this to falling birth rates. Fertility is falling for its own reasons, mostly housing and the cost of raising children, and sex frequency within couples who want children isn't really the constraint.
SAM: So why does this belong on a markets and business show?
ALEX: 3 reasons. First, household formation. A rich world where more people live alone needs more housing units per head, smaller units, and more household energy demand per person, because 1 person heating a flat uses more than half of what 2 do. That's real money for builders and utilities. Second, the loneliness economy, everything from dating apps, whose user growth has stalled, to companionship products, including AI companions, which are booming precisely in this gap. Third, politics. A generation that is less coupled, less likely to own a home and more online has different politics, and you'll hear a version of that argument in our op-eds segment.
SAM: And the thread I'd pull is the measurement one. Here we have a slow social shift that's being measured for the first time in a decade, with instruments that are themselves getting worse. The bill for falling survey response rates comes due exactly when you need the data most.
ALEX: That's right. A sexual recession you can only half-measure is a good metaphor for a lot of what we cover.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: Energy, and the theme this week is not whether data centers pay for the grid, it's how. We start in Wyoming. On Tuesday, Cheyenne Light, Fuel and Power, the Black Hills Energy subsidiary, filed 3 facility expansion agreements at FERC under docket ER27-634. Microsoft pays for the transmission and distribution buildout for 3 planned Wyoming data centers through contribution-in-aid-of-construction payments.
ALEX: The dollar figures are redacted, but the interesting part is that the utility says FERC has no jurisdiction over these retail agreements and filed anyway, citing the commission's focus on who pays for large-load transmission. That's defensive filing: get the deal on the record at FERC now, so nobody can later argue in a show-cause or a cost-allocation complaint that the residential base subsidized Microsoft's wires. The load will be served under Cheyenne's Large Power Contract Service tariff plus a proposed Large Customer Transmission Cost Adjustment Mechanism, and the pipeline behind it is about 3 gigawatts near-term, including 600 megawatts for Microsoft and Meta, with another 2.5 gigawatts in early talks. Black Hills' 10-Q also shows a Google deal signed in September.
SAM: Second, Ohio, where on November 19 Google and AEP Ohio jointly asked the Public Utilities Commission to approve Google paying the full $10.8 million incremental cost of advanced conductors on about 15.5 miles of 50-year-old lines in Columbus, as part of a rebuild AEP already had planned.
ALEX: What Google buys with that $10.8 million is time: 24 megawatts of already-contracted capacity about 2 years early. The conductor is CTC Global's carbon-core ACCC, which carries roughly twice the current of standard steel-reinforced wire on the same towers. And once the Central Ohio 765-kilovolt buildout lands, the upgraded lines are expected to free up more than 700 megawatts for everyone. It's billed as a first-of-its-kind corporate-funded upgrade on a regulated grid. It still needs both PUCO and the Ohio Power Siting Board, with in-service targeted for late 2028. If it's approved, every hyperscaler with a stalled interconnection will ask its utility for the same menu: pay for the reconductor, jump the timeline.
SAM: And the opposite strategy, also in Ohio. The same day, the Ohio Power Siting Board approved PowerConneX's Ashville Energy Center in Pickaway County, up to 800 megawatts of gas generation, with 41 conditions. PowerConneX is an EdgeConneX affiliate, and the plant is fully islanded, with no grid connection at all, feeding only the adjacent EdgeConneX campus, with commercial operation as early as the second quarter of 2027.
ALEX: Islanding dodges every fight we just described: no queue, no cost allocation, no reconductoring. What it doesn't dodge is local politics. Residents forced a referendum, and the risk moves from the PJM queue to the township hall.
SAM: Then Washington. The Senate passed the data-center cost allocation bill 62 to 33 on November 19, and there's now a price tag: Grid Strategies estimates about $55 billion over 10 years in data-center cost allocation, $35 billion of embedded costs and $20 billion incremental, on roughly 40.3 gigawatts of non-ERCOT data-center interconnections. The House is expected to take it up right after Thanksgiving, and any change sends it back to the Senate before January 3.
ALEX: The $35 billion of embedded costs is the bit to watch. That's recovery of grid investment already in rate base, assigned to new large loads. If that survives the House, it becomes a federal floor under every state's large-load tariff, and it changes the all-in cost per megawatt for every project in your pipeline.
SAM: And a quick one from Canada: Ontario's grid operator, the IESO, presented draft market rules that would make data centers and other computational loads above 10 megawatts demonstrate they meet reliability standards before they enter regular service.
ALEX: 10 megawatts, against FERC's large-load definition at 50 megawatts and above 69 kilovolts. That makes Ontario the strictest compute gate in the region, which matters for cross-border siting. So the week's arc is that the question of who pays has become a menu: prepay the wires like Microsoft, buy the upgrade like Google, skip the grid like EdgeConneX, or have Congress write the bill for you.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Mandarin now, 2 words for our China story. First, 不至于, bú zhìyú. It's an abstract connective-adverb meaning won't go so far as, or not as bad as. You use it to put a ceiling on how bad something gets.
ALEX: Register is neutral, slightly spoken, but you'll see it constantly in market commentary. The near-synonym to contrast is 未必, which means not necessarily, a judgment about probability. 不至于 is about degree, a judgment about how far. Collocations: 不至于违约, won't go as far as default, and 还不至于, not yet at the point of. Here's an example.
SAM: 公司的现金很紧张，但还不至于违约。(The company's cash is very tight, but it won't go so far as default.)
ALEX: And a softer, everyday one. 他只是累了，不至于生气。(He's just tired; it's not as bad as being angry.)
SAM: Second word: 拖延, tuōyán, to drag out or stall. It's neutral to negative, and the contrast is with 推迟, which means to postpone and is a neutral rescheduling. 拖延 implies foot-dragging, delay as a tactic.
ALEX: It pairs with 还款, repayment, as in 拖延还款, and with 时间, as in 拖延时间, playing for time. You'll often see it with 一再, repeatedly.
SAM: 会议推迟到下周了。(The meeting has been postponed to next week.)
ALEX: 开发商一再拖延还款，债权人越来越不满。(The developer keeps dragging out repayment, and creditors are increasingly unhappy.) Listen for both in the next story.
