## SEGMENT ONE — HEADLINES
SAM: Israel's public broadcaster Kan reported on Thursday that Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party and United Torah Judaism have agreed an initial framework for a Haredi draft law. The outline has no enlistment quotas, adds oversight of yeshiva exemptions, and creates new service tracks. UTJ has hired the attorney Boaz Ben Tzur to draft it. Naftali Bennett, whose bloc Eisenkot is meant to be aligned with, is unhappy and has pushed his own talks with Yashar back to Sunday. The same day, dozens of Haredi protesters blocked the entrance to Ben Gurion Airport over the arrest of draft evaders; police declared the protest illegal and cleared it by about 10 at night, with no arrests.
ALEX: The missing word in that framework is quotas. Every serious draft proposal since the High Court ruling has hinged on numbers with penalties attached, because exemptions without targets are just the status quo with a new name. So the deal Eisenkot is shopping buys Haredi votes in a future coalition at the cost of the one mechanism that would actually put people in uniform. That's why Bennett is balking: the draft is the issue his voters care most about, and a framework without numbers is a headline without a ledger. And the airport blockade tells you the street will fight even the soft version.
SAM: To Kansas, where Nidal Hasan, who killed 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009, has asked a federal court to stay his execution by firing squad, scheduled for December 3 at 1 p.m. Central at Fort Hood. It would be the American military's first firing-squad execution since 1945. Judge Mattivi ordered the government to respond by today and set a hearing for next week, the ACLU of Kansas is acting as standby counsel, and President Trump has cancelled the livestream that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had proposed.
ALEX: The legal question is narrow, mostly about method and procedure after Hasan waived most of his appeals, but the precedent is large. The military justice system has not carried out any execution in more than 6 decades, and doing it by firing squad, on a base, with a livestream briefly on the table, turns a sentence into a statement. The cancellation of the livestream is the tell that the White House sees the risk.

## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Our deep dive comes from The Economist, from the This Week edition in your inbox. The headline: after a kingpin's arrest, Sinaloa's drug war could get worse. The blurb reads, drone-delivered bombs and landmines add to the slaughter. We've rebuilt the substance from open reporting, so here is the story underneath.
ALEX: On November 20 Mexican forces captured Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as El Chapo Isidro, in Guasave in northern Sinaloa. He ran the Guasave cartel and was on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted list. The operation was violent: at least 11 people dead, 4 soldiers and 7 gunmen, and at least 10 roadblocks set up around the city afterwards. 7 others were arrested with him, including his alleged number 2 and a finance operator. Mexico says U.S. intelligence helped, but he is being tried in Mexico, not extradited, and he entered the Altiplano maximum-security prison on November 23.
SAM: And the reason The Economist thinks this makes things worse rather than better is who he was allied with.
ALEX: Right. Sinaloa has been at war with itself since September 2024, when Ismael El Mayo Zambada was abducted to the United States. On one side is La Mayiza, the faction loyal to the Zambada family, now led by Zambada Sicairos. On the other are the Chapitos, the sons of Joaquín Guzmán, led by Iván Archivaldo and Jesús Alfredo. Analysts at InSight Crime argue that Chapo Isidro's alliance with La Mayiza was what had tipped the war their way, pushing the Chapitos south toward Concordia, Rosario and Escuinapa. Take him out, and the Mexican paper La Jornada's headline was simply, La Mayiza weakens. And on Thursday authorities arrested another Mayiza figure, Fernando Félix Corrales, known as El Chapo Félix, from Zambada Sicairos's inner circle.
SAM: So the state has just knocked out the side that was winning.
ALEX: That's the argument. Analysts quoted by Infobae this week expect the Chapitos to counter-attack to win back territory, and the way this war is fought has changed. On October 12 soldiers in Sinaloa seized 48 drones, 6 landmines and 36 homemade bombs in one operation. A U.S. counter-terrorism research centre, NCITE, has counted 221 cartel drone attacks nationwide between 2021 and 2025, and AP has reported cartels turning to car bombs and improvised explosives. That's the slaughter in the blurb: the war is becoming more military in its methods, not just more frequent.
SAM: Put a number on the toll.
ALEX: State data compiled by Uno TV counts roughly 3,724 homicides in Sinaloa from September 9, 2024 to September 7 this year, about 5 a day. A collective of searching mothers, Sabuesos Guerreras, counts about 5,800 people disappeared since mid-2024, and they say that's an undercount. Now, the contested part. ACLED, the conflict-data project, found violence in Sinaloa from January to August this year down 29% on the year before. President Sheinbaum has leaned on numbers like that. But she has also conceded that homicides rose in September and October, and the International Crisis Group says the fighting hasn't stopped, it has moved, out of Culiacán and into rural areas and the port of Mazatlán.
SAM: So the decline is partly a map problem.
ALEX: Partly. If violence leaves the state capital, where it's counted and covered, and spreads to the countryside, the aggregate can fall while the war widens. And this is the larger debate about the kingpin strategy. Researchers at New Lines Institute and the Stimson Center argue that decapitation breeds splinters: you remove a boss, his lieutenants fight over the succession, rivals probe the weakness, and violence spikes. Mexico has seen this play out all year. El Mencho, head of the Jalisco cartel, was killed in February, violence flared in more than 20 states, and his successor, known as El Jardinero, was caught in April.
SAM: And Washington has just added a new element. On Wednesday DEA Administrator Terry Cole announced anonymous tip lines on Signal, WhatsApp and email aimed at people inside Sinaloa. On Thursday Sheinbaum called that interference and cited an AP finding that the DEA had left at least 1.4 million counterfeit fentanyl pills unseized in a past operation, calling it the Fast and Furious of fentanyl. Mexico's security cabinet said it does not accept unilateral actions.
ALEX: Which is the second-order read. The arrest of Chapo Isidro gives both governments something: Washington gets a Ten Most Wanted name and can say its intelligence delivered, which feeds the cartels-as-terrorists framing; Mexico gets to try him at home, a sovereignty signal. But the DEA tip lines go straight around the Mexican state, recruiting informants inside its territory, and that's exactly the kind of move that makes Sheinbaum's government less willing to share. The arrest is the headline; what decides whether Sinaloa gets calmer is whether the two governments keep cooperating after it, and whether anyone has a plan for the vacuum.
SAM: What should we watch?
ALEX: Three things. Mazatlán, because a tourist port turning into a front line changes the economic cost of this war overnight. The southern municipalities, where a Chapitos counter-offensive would show up first. And the drone and landmine seizures, because if those keep climbing, the conflict is no longer gang violence in any meaningful sense, and that would test Sheinbaum's insistence that the strategy hasn't changed.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: Energy, and today the theme is counting only what's real: signed, approved, or running. Start with Latitude Intelligence's new report, Terms of Power 2. It finds utilities' proposed carbon-free projects would cover only about 30% of the annual energy their signed data-center load needs. Count only projects regulators have actually approved, and it drops to 15%. About 120 terawatt-hours, 25% of the need, has no planned supply of any kind, rising to 40% if you count only approved projects. And signed data-center load is about 123 gigawatts, against more than 500 gigawatts of reported requests.
ALEX: That last ratio is the one to remember. We've talked before about the gap between requests and signed load; this report takes the next step and asks what supply is actually lined up for the load that is real. The answer is that the gap gets filled by default, with gas or market purchases, inside utility resource plans the hyperscalers don't control. Every 24/7 carbon-free pledge sitting on a hyperscaler's sustainability page now depends on an IRP in Georgia or Virginia that may never include the clean megawatts. Hourly matching on paper and gas on the wire.
SAM: And a new study puts a number on what that looks like today. A Harvard-led paper in Nature Sustainability mapped 403 U.S. hyperscale data centers and estimates they used between 68 and 99 terawatt-hours from May 2024 to April 2025, with a central estimate of about 82, roughly 1.8% of U.S. load and about what the state of Michigan uses in a year. 54% of that supply was fossil, 38% gas and 15% coal, for about 25.7 million tonnes of CO2. Virginia, Oregon, Ohio and Iowa account for more than half.
ALEX: The value of this paper is that it's bottom-up and site-level, not a top-down estimate from capex. It tells you the grid mix the facilities actually draw on, which is different from the clean energy their owners buy on paper through certificates. And it will show up in large-load tariff cases, because intervenors now have a peer-reviewed, location-specific figure to put in front of commissions.
SAM: Then the gas build-out itself. A Sierra Club report counts about 250 gigawatts of gas planned or under construction to serve data centers, and says the 12 largest on-site projects alone total more than 45 gigawatts, more than all the gas capacity the U.S. has built since 2019, with up to 680 million tonnes of additional CO2. But the tracker Cleanview counts 108 behind-the-meter gas plants, about 116 gigawatts announced, and only about 2 gigawatts actually operating.
ALEX: 116 announced, 2 running. That gap is turbine slots and fuel supply. The big three turbine makers are sold out into the next decade, aeroderivatives and recips are filling some of the hole, and pipeline laterals to a remote campus take years. So expect the planned figure to shrink hard as projects that announced without a turbine reservation quietly die. And expect the opposition to move to air permits, because a behind-the-meter plant still needs one, and that's the cleanest legal choke point.
SAM: On the cost side, utilities have filed their proposed contract terms at FERC in the large-load proceedings. In PJM, utilities want data centers to commit to pay for at least 80% of the transmission service they request for at least 10 years, with collateral starting at 25% and rising to 100% by the in-service date, and an exit fee if they leave early. CAISO and NYISO utilities propose 15 years, and MISO a 3-way agreement among utility, data center and MISO of up to 20 years. Comments are due December 7, and PJM wants a ruling within 120 days.
ALEX: That's the mechanism that turns a load forecast into a balance-sheet item. Today a speculative developer can hold a queue position with a small deposit. Under these terms, the developer posts collateral that climbs to the full value of the upgrade, so the phantom load we keep talking about either finances itself or exits. It's the same discipline as Latitude's report, only enforced with letters of credit instead of spreadsheets.
SAM: And politics. Michigan Democrats won a full trifecta in the midterms, and Governor-elect Jocelyn Benson takes office in January. Advocates expect a pause on new data-center construction until rules on cost, land, water and disclosure are in place, and bills already filed would pause development until April 1, 2027. DTE has 2.4 gigawatts committed, including Oracle and OpenAI and Google.
ALEX: Michigan is the first state where the midterms could flip data-center policy outright. A pause probably needs the legislature, not just the governor, but even the threat puts DTE and Consumers timelines at risk, and every developer with a Great Lakes site is now pricing a political discount. If affordability was the campaign message, a moratorium is the cheapest way to show it.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Our 2 Mandarin words are tied to today's China story. The first is 集中, jízhōng, to concentrate, or concentrated. It's a verb and an adjective, and in economic writing it's the go-to word for where gains or risks pile up.
ALEX: The collocation pattern is 集中在 plus a place or sector: concentrated in. Here's a finance-desk example. 今年的利润主要集中在高科技行业。(This year's profits are mainly concentrated in high-tech industries.) It pairs with abstract nouns too: 集中精力, to focus your energy, and 风险集中, concentration risk.
SAM: The near-synonym contrast is 聚集. 聚集 is physical, people or things gathering in one place: 人群在广场聚集。(A crowd gathered in the square.) 集中 is the abstract, analytical version, and it's also used for deliberate action, like 集中资源, pooling resources. Register is neutral, equally at home in a newspaper and a meeting.
ALEX: The second word is 恰恰, qiàqià, precisely, exactly, and it usually carries a sense of contrast: precisely the opposite of what you'd expect. It's more formal and more emphatic than 正好, which just means it happens to fit. Its most famous collocation is 恰恰相反, quite the contrary.
SAM: Another pattern is 恰恰说明, which shows exactly that. 利润大涨恰恰说明消费还很弱。(The profit surge shows precisely that consumption is still weak.) And in an argument: 问题恰恰在这里。(That is exactly where the problem lies.) So 正好 is a coincidence; 恰恰 is a point you're making.
