## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Which is exactly where today's Deep Dive lives. Adam Tooze published a new Chartbook yesterday — number 469 — on what he's calling the risk of unwind in the Treasury market, and it's the plumbing underneath everything we've been covering on yields the last two weeks.
ALEX: His argument starts with what's changed. The old story of who buys US Treasuries was foreign central banks recycling their trade surpluses back into dollar debt — the "Bretton Woods 2.0" mechanism. That's largely collapsed since 2008, and China specifically is now actively diversifying away from Treasuries even as its trade surplus stays enormous.
SAM: So who's actually buying now?
ALEX: Leveraged hedge funds, and Tooze cites new NY Fed research from economist Phillip Monin showing hedge funds' Treasury holdings now exceed those of mutual funds AND US-chartered depository institutions combined. That's a real structural handoff, not a marginal shift.
SAM: But hedge funds buying Treasuries with their own capital is one thing —
ALEX: — and that's the second half of the argument, it isn't their own capital. Office of Financial Research data show hedge-fund repo borrowing up 154% since 2022, prime-brokerage borrowing up 83%. Nearly half of hedge funds' 2.4-trillion-dollar long Treasury position as of last September is leveraged arbitrage — mostly the basis trade, arbitraging cash Treasuries against futures.
SAM: The basis trade — that's the same mechanism from March 2020.
ALEX: Exactly the one — Tooze's prior post, Chartbook 468, walks through the actual Sunday emergency FOMC call that March, when the basis trade unwind nearly broke the Treasury market and needed roughly 75 billion dollars a day of emergency Fed purchases to stop it. Basis-trade volume today is around 830 billion dollars — nearly double its early-2020 peak — now 3.5% of privately-held Treasuries by value versus a 2.5% peak back then. That's a 40% jump in concentration risk sitting in the exact same trade that broke once already.
SAM: So the "safest, most liquid market in the world" is now structurally dependent on leveraged, rate-sensitive positioning.
ALEX: That's the read, and it's why Friday's quiet tape and the yield moves we've tracked for two weeks — the 30-year hitting 5.28%, Bessent doubling the buyback program — aren't separate stories. They're all downstream of the same fragile plumbing. If that leverage has to unwind fast, "quiet" can turn into March 2020 very quickly, and there's more of it sitting there now than there was then.
SAM: What would actually trigger a fast unwind, rather than just sitting there as a risk?
ALEX: The basis trade only works while the cash-futures spread stays predictable and repo funding stays cheap. A sharp funding-cost spike, or a margin call that forces funds to sell the cash leg faster than they can unwind the futures leg, is exactly what happened in March 2020 — and it doesn't need a Treasury-specific shock to start, just a liquidity squeeze anywhere that forces deleveraging. Tooze's point is that the 40% jump in concentration since then means the same trigger now has a bigger position to unwind through a market that hasn't gotten any deeper.
SAM: And the Fed's the backstop either way.
ALEX: It is, but that's Bessent's own dilemma — the buyback program he doubled two weeks ago is partly an attempt to manage exactly this fragility before it needs an emergency response, not after. Whether direct Treasury purchases from the demand side can substitute for hedge-fund leverage at this scale is, genuinely, untested.
SAM: How does this connect back to what China's doing with Evergrande later in the show?
ALEX: It's the same underlying question from two different angles — who's actually willing to hold long-term debt when the official, central-bank-driven buyer base thins out. China's dealing with it in real estate, where the answer has been years of extend-and-pretend before this week's forced reckoning. The US version is still in the "extend" phase, with leveraged funds standing in for the buyers who used to show up automatically — Tooze's whole point is that phase doesn't last forever either.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: On to Mandarin. First phrase — 无论……都…….
ALEX: 无论……都……, wúlùn... dōu... — "no matter what, always." It's 书面语, formal written register, and it's the more considered cousin of the more colloquial 不管……都……. Where 不管 reads like an everyday judgment — whatever happens, same result — 无论 reads like something you'd hear from a news anchor, deliberate and analytical rather than casual.
SAM: Second — 了结.
ALEX: 了结, liǎojié — to conclude, to wrap up, to formally settle. Also 书面语, an abstract verb that collocates with 案件, a case, 纠纷, a dispute, or 债务, debt. It's distinct from 解决, to resolve — 解决 implies the underlying problem actually got fixed, while 了结 just means the matter was brought to a definitive close, resolved or not.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY
SAM: Op-eds — first up, Matthew Yglesias in Slow Boring, arguing for giving people what they actually want instead of data centers.

SAM: Second, The Economist's leader asking whether AIs could become conscious.
ALEX: Their steelman isn't metaphysical — even if no AI is conscious in any deep sense, a growing number of people already treat chatbots as sentient, from relationships to what's being called AI psychosis. SAM: And the pushback?
ALEX: Critics say the piece leans on an outdated premise — that only living things can be conscious — sidestepping decades of functionalist philosophy-of-mind work. SAM: Which side of that do you actually buy?
ALEX: The precautionary case for disclosure rules stands on its own regardless of the consciousness question — you don't need to resolve philosophy of mind to justify telling people they're talking to software. SAM: It's a strange split, prioritizing a philosophical question over an economic one that's already showing up in the data.
ALEX: It is — one piece is asking whether the technology might eventually deserve moral consideration, and the harder question is what happens to the labor market and financial stability while everyone's still debating that. The urgent policy conversation is the second one, and it's getting a fraction of the attention.
