## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
SAM: For the deep dive, we're going somewhere completely different. Last week's Economist Today newsletter flagged a long read from 1843 magazine by William Ralston: inside the criminal gangs making fake wine. The argument in a line: wine fraud is ancient, but counterfeiting has industrialised, and it's moving down-market, from trophy Burgundy to supermarket bottles.
ALEX: The full piece is paywalled, so we'll be careful: we're taking the thesis from the Economist's own summary and rebuilding the substance from the public record. And the public record is wild. The case that defines the genre is what the French called the Red Bicyclette affair. Between 2006 and 2008, a group of Languedoc producers and dealers sold E and J Gallo roughly 18 million bottles' worth of wine labelled as Pinot Noir for its Red Bicyclette brand. It was mostly cheaper Merlot and Syrah.
SAM: And nobody tasted the difference. That's the part that should bother you. It was caught by paperwork: inspectors noticed the suppliers were selling more Pinot Noir than they could plausibly have bought or grown. A court in Carcassonne convicted 12 people in 2010, mostly suspended sentences and fines.
ALEX: Compare that to the famous end of the market. Rudy Kurniawan, the collector who blended old Bordeaux and Burgundy in his California kitchen and relabelled them as legendary vintages, sold tens of millions of dollars of fakes to sophisticated buyers before the FBI arrested him in 2012. He got 10 years. One veteran authentication expert, Maureen Downey, argues the total value of his fakes still circulating is far higher than what was prosecuted.
SAM: Then in 2024, Europol coordinated a French, Italian and Swiss operation against a ring selling fake Burgundy at up to 15,000 euros a bottle. 6 arrests, more than 2 million euros in profit. That's the trophy end, still very much alive.
ALEX: But here's where the 1843 thesis gets interesting. The economics of fraud flip as you go down-market. At 15,000 euros a bottle you have to fool a connoisseur, an auction specialist and a provenance file. At 6 pounds a bottle you don't have to fool anyone, because nobody's checking. The margin comes from volume and from evading excise duty, not from deception at the point of taste.
SAM: So who's doing it? The piece's claim, as summarised, is organised criminal networks, the same groups that move counterfeit spirits, cigarettes and pharmaceuticals, adding wine because it's lower risk and the penalties are trivial. Selling 41 fake bottles in a British corner shop costs you a licence review, not a prison sentence.
ALEX: And that's the second-order point. Enforcement in Britain sits largely with local trading standards and the Food Standards Agency's food crime unit, not with customs or serious-crime police. A supermarket brand doesn't have the incentive a Pétrus or a Romanée-Conti does to spend on anti-counterfeiting, because each fake bottle costs it a few pounds of lost sale and a lot of reputational risk it can't measure.
SAM: What's contested? First, scale. You'll see a figure that 20% of internationally traded wine is fraudulent. It's quoted constantly and sourced weakly. Downey's estimate for the secondary fine-wine market is more like 5 to 10%. Nobody has a credible number for supermarket wine, which is part of the problem.
ALEX: Second, harm. If 18 million bottles of Merlot went out as Pinot and nobody noticed, who was hurt? The honest answer is the legitimate producers who were underpriced, the brand that carried the liability, and the tax authorities. That's a fraud economy, not a health scandal, until it isn't. Italy has had cases of cheap wine adulterated with industrial chemicals, and that's where counterfeit spirits have historically killed people.
SAM: Third, the fix. The top estates now use holograms, sealed capsules with per-bottle codes, and special inks. That's affordable on a 1,000-euro bottle and ruinous on a 6-pound one. So the real question is whether bottle-level traceability, the kind the EU already mandates for tobacco, eventually gets pushed onto mass-market wine.
ALEX: And this ties to something we talk about a lot on this show without meaning to. Supply chains that are trusted because nobody looks closely. The Red Bicyclette case was caught by an accountant reconciling volumes, not a sommelier. The same is true in energy, in chips, in carbon credits: when the product is indistinguishable at the point of use, the audit trail is the product.
SAM: My takeaway as a consumer: the risk isn't the expensive bottle you buy at auction, where provenance is documented. It's the unfamiliar case of a famous label at a price that's slightly too good, from a seller who isn't the brand's distributor.
ALEX: And as an investor, if you own a drinks brand, counterfeit exposure is now an operating risk, not a footnote. A good piece. It's a 1843 long read via The Economist, worth your weekend.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: Energy, and the theme this week is turbine slots changing hands. SoftBank booked a 597.2-billion-yen quarterly loss, roughly 3.7 billion dollars, tied to US data-center power equipment, through a subsidiary called Silver Bands 7.
ALEX: According to the Wall Street Journal's reporting, SoftBank reserved more than 13 gigawatts of gas turbines between late 2025 and early 2026, then cancelled much of it as project specifications changed. CFO Yoshimitsu Goto told analysts the cancellations were "a commercial decision, not a default," a direct rebuttal of Elon Musk's post on X suggesting otherwise, and said the loss is unrelated to its Ohio project.
SAM: The 13-gigawatt figure is press reporting, not the filing, so treat the size as indicative. But the direction matters more.
ALEX: It does. This is the first visible crack in speculative turbine reservations. Through 2025, slot reservations for heavy-duty gas turbines traded almost like options: put down a deposit, hold a place in a 5-to-7-year queue, sell or assign later. If a buyer with SoftBank's balance sheet is walking away from slots and eating the loss, those positions go back to the OEMs or onto the secondary market. Watch for lead-time quotes from GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi easing at the margin over the next 2 quarters. Not a collapse, but the first loosening since the queue formed.
SAM: And on the other side of the same trade, the Financial Times reports Anthropic has taken over OnePWR Solutions' order book for Caterpillar gas generators, about 1.7 gigawatts for around 3.4 billion dollars, including the engineering and installation capability, but without buying the company or hiring its staff. Delivery over roughly 2 years.
ALEX: Do the maths: that's about 2,000 dollars a kilowatt all-in for reciprocating gensets. Expensive per kilowatt against a combined-cycle plant, but you're paying to skip a 6-year turbine queue and get firm on-site power in 24 months. There's a gap in the record, too: in February OnePWR was describing about 1.2 gigawatts, 400 megawatts in stock plus 800 over 24 months, and nobody has explained the step-up to 1.7. Sites aren't disclosed; one report says Texas.
SAM: Put those 2 together and it's the same week: a speculator gives up slots, an end user pays a premium for modular capacity. That's a market maturing from hoarding to deployment.
ALEX: Third item, from DealFlow's weekly in your inbox. Panamint Capital closed a 769-million-dollar first phase of a 1.8-billion-dollar green loan for Big Rooter West, 491 megawatts DC of solar on the Twin Oaks coal plant and Calvert Mine site in Robertson County, Texas. SAM: The structure: a 240-million-dollar construction-to-term loan, a 461-million-dollar tax-equity bridge and a 68-million-dollar letter of credit. The east phase is 658 megawatts DC, over 1 billion dollars, closing in the first quarter of 2027, with one investment-grade offtaker.
ALEX: 2 reads. DealFlow's own question is why the tax-equity bridge is nearly twice the term loan, which tells you how much of the capital stack is still leaning on transferable credits under the post-2025 rules. And the site: a retiring coal plant comes with an existing interconnection. Brownfield thermal sites are becoming the cleanest way around the ERCOT queue, for solar now, and for data centers next.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
ALEX: Mandarin now, and 2 words that live on the financial pages. The first is 有所. It means "to some extent" or "somewhat," and it's distinctly formal, 书面语. You'll find it in central-bank statements, statistics releases and analyst notes, almost never in conversation.
SAM: The collocation pattern is the key. 有所 sits in front of a 2-syllable verb: 有所增长, 有所下降, 有所回升, 有所改善. You can't put it in front of a 1-syllable verb or a bare adjective, which is where learners trip up.
ALEX: Contrast it with 有点 or 稍微, which are spoken and take adjectives: 有点贵, 稍微好一点. 有所 is also a hedging device. When an official report says something 有所改善, it's telling you it improved, but not by much, and they'd rather you didn't ask how much.
SAM: 今年的零售销售有所增长，但是力度不大。(Retail sales grew somewhat this year, but not with much strength.)
ALEX: The second word is 迟迟. It means "slow to happen" or "long delayed," and it carries a note of frustrated expectation. You've been waiting for something, and it keeps not arriving.
SAM: The pattern is almost always 迟迟 plus a negative: 迟迟不, or 迟迟没有, followed by the verb. 迟迟没有回暖, 迟迟不肯表态, 迟迟未能落地. It works in both formal writing and educated speech.
ALEX: The near-synonym is 一直, which is neutral and just means continuously. 一直没有回暖 is a fact. 迟迟没有回暖 is a fact plus the sense that it should have recovered by now. And 始终 is more formal, from start to finish, without the impatience.
SAM: 房价迟迟没有企稳，家庭还是不敢花钱。(Home prices have been slow to stabilise, so households still don't dare to spend.)
ALEX: 有所 and 迟迟: one hedges the good news, the other complains about the delay. Both are about to show up in China.
