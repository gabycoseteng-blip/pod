## SEGMENT TWO — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
ALEX: This is where the micro reckoning gets physical, and the single best data point this week came out of PG&E's quarter — because it tells you the shape of AI demand is changing, not just the size.
SAM: Give me the number.
ALEX: PG&E's data-center interconnection pipeline hit 12.7 gigawatts, up from 5.1 gigawatts just one quarter ago. That's a two-and-a-half-times jump in a single quarter. But CEO Patti Poppe said the thing that actually matters: they're focusing, quote, not on size, but on quality — and most of the new applications are now 100 megawatts or smaller. Only about 1.8 gigawatts of that pipeline is expected online by 2030.
SAM: So the pipeline exploded, but the individual loads shrank.
ALEX: That's the tell, and it's a regime change. The single-campus, gigawatt training whale is being joined — maybe displaced — by a swarm of smaller, latency-sited inference nodes. Inference wants to sit close to users, so you get many sub-100-megawatt requests scattered across the map instead of one giant one. And that reshapes everything downstream: interconnection study queues, and crucially, it lets a utility cherry-pick creditworthy loads instead of betting its whole rate base on a single hyperscaler off-taker. Smaller, more numerous, more diversifiable.
SAM: You've also got a genuinely new kind of constraint surfacing this week — not megawatts, but the behavior of the megawatts.
ALEX: This is my favorite item of the week. Crusoe and ON.energy are deploying 5 gigawatts of medium-voltage — call it an AI uninterruptible power system — across hyperscale campuses, commissioning through 2026 into 2027. It's an inline power-electronics layer that decouples the facility from the grid and rides through voltage faults without disconnecting. But the real job is taming load volatility.
SAM: Explain the volatility problem, because I don't think people picture it.
ALEX: A large AI training cluster doesn't draw steady power. When the GPUs synchronize on a training step, the load can swing from high to low and back hundreds, even thousands of times a day. To a utility, a gigawatt of training doesn't look like a gigawatt of steady factory load — it looks like a gigawatt that's constantly slamming up and down, which is genuinely destabilizing to the local grid. So this buffering layer is becoming a gating requirement to interconnect at all.
SAM: And the investable read?
ALEX: It opens a whole new behind-the-meter equipment category, and it means grid planners will start pricing ride-through capability — not just raw capacity. You don't just have to prove you can supply the electrons; you have to prove your load won't wreck the neighborhood. That's a new bar, and a new margin pool. Third item, and it ties straight to Wednesday and Thursday: SemiAnalysis reports that Nvidia's next platform, Vera Rubin, delivers roughly 5.4 times the performance per megawatt of the current generation on inference workloads.
SAM: 5.4 times per megawatt — if it holds.
ALEX: Big if — that number is off CoreWeave engineering samples, SemiAnalysis says it hasn't independently verified it, and audited figures come in Q3. But take it seriously as a direction. A five-fold jump in tokens-per-megawatt is the first real efficiency valve on the electrons wall. A fixed substation feed suddenly buys you five times the inference output next cycle. And that cuts two ways — it could blunt near-term megawatt demand, or, by Jevons paradox, it could pull even more compute onto the same interconnection because compute just got cheaper to power.
SAM: And that's the lens for the capex prints.
ALEX: Precisely. When Microsoft and Meta guide Wednesday and Amazon guides Thursday, the question isn't just how many billions. It's whether their capex plans assume this efficiency step is coming — in which case the raw-megawatt panic is overdone — or whether they're still provisioning power as if every token needs today's wattage. That single assumption sits under every gigawatt in every one of these pipelines.

## SEGMENT FIVE — ARTS & CULTURE
SAM: Arts and culture. Alex, let's start where the eye goes — with two faces, five centuries apart.
ALEX: Two portrait stories, and the first is a quiet coup for a tiny Sicilian museum. In Cefalù, the Museo Mandralisca has taken its most famous possession — Antonello da Messina's "Portrait of an Unknown Man," painted around 1465 — and placed it face to face with a second Antonello portrait, on loan from the Palazzo Madama in Turin. Two of the sharpest psychological studies in all of Quattrocento painting, in one small room.
SAM: And Antonello's the painter who bridged two worlds.
ALEX: That's why it matters. Antonello fused Flemish oil technique — that jewel-like precision, the way light sits on skin — with Italian monumentality and structure. His "Unknown Man" has this faint, knowing half-smile that people have argued about for 150 years. Seeing two of these heads in dialogue lets you read the artist's mind: how he builds a living, skeptical human being out of oil and light. For a regional museum to stage that is a real critical event, not a listings entry.
SAM: Story two keeps us on the human face but jumps to the 20th century.
ALEX: The National Portrait Gallery in London has "Marilyn Monroe: A Portrait," marking the centenary of her birth, on through early September. ALEX: Exactly — it's a show about authorship and control, which is a far more interesting subject than celebrity. ## SEGMENT SEVEN — ONE GOOD THING
ALEX: One good thing, and it's a genuine advance in men's health. Imperial College London reported the results of a 10-year trial following about 3,500 men with prostate cancer — and it found that focal therapy, which targets only the tumor rather than treating the whole prostate, is as effective as full surgery or radiotherapy at controlling the cancer.
SAM: As effective — but easier on the patient?
ALEX: That's the breakthrough. Treating the whole gland is what causes the two side effects men dread most: incontinence and erectile dysfunction. By treating only the diseased tissue, focal therapy roughly halved both. Same cancer control, about half the life-altering harm — and now with a decade of data behind it, which is what turns a promising idea into a standard of care.
SAM: So it's not a trade-off anymore — you don't have to choose between beating it and living well after.
ALEX: That's the whole point. For years the deal was: we can treat this, but it may cost you your continence or your sex life. A 10-year study of 3,500 men says, increasingly, that's a false choice. For the hundreds of thousands of men diagnosed every year, that's a very good thing to wake up to.

ALEX: And I'm Alex. Whatever this Monday holds, take good care of yourself, and we'll be right back here with you tomorrow.
