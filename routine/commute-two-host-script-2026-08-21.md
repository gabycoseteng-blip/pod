## SEGMENT — DEEP DIVE
SAM: Let's go deep on one thing, and this is from Adam Tooze's Chartbook — issue 466 — a genuinely strange story about a rock in a German river.
ALEX: Specifically the Rhine, at a town called Kaub. The Rhine carries 80% of Germany's roughly 223 million tons of inland-shipped cargo a year, and the single tightest point in the entire system is a submerged rock ledge at Kaub, where the channel runs about 20 centimeters shallower than the rest of the river.
SAM: Twenty centimeters doesn't sound like much.
ALEX: It's everything, in low water — barges either have to lighten their loads or stop moving entirely once the river drops. And here's the part that makes it a real story: the German government promised to remove those rocks back in 1992, under Kohl. 34 years later, it's still not scheduled to finish until 2033 — even though shipping-industry executives say the actual engineering work is about eight months.
SAM: 34 years for an eight-month job.
ALEX: Which is exactly why ING's global macro head, Carsten Brzeski, calls Kaub "Germany's Strait of Hormuz" — a chokepoint that's genuinely load-bearing for the whole economy, sitting unfixed for a generation.
SAM: So why hasn't it been fixed, if the engineering is that fast?
ALEX: This is the part Tooze goes deep on, and it's genuinely contested, not just bureaucratic drag. Dredging the rock out is the fast, politically attractive fix — but hydrologists point out it doesn't add any water to the system. It actually speeds outflow to the sea, which makes downstream shortages worse in future dry years. The water agency's preferred fix is different — low-water groynes that narrow the channel to concentrate the water that's already there, rather than let more of it run out to sea faster.
SAM: What's the actual economic stake if Kaub stays broken?
ALEX: BASF's Ludwigshafen site — the largest chemical complex in the world — sits right on the Rhine and depends on barge deliveries for feedstock. In low-water years, it's already had to shift toward more expensive rail and truck substitution, and that cost shows up in German industrial competitiveness broadly, not just in shipping-company margins.
SAM: That's a real tradeoff, not just political inertia.
ALEX: Exactly, and it's why federal funding has been so thin — 7 million euros for a sediment-replenishment project elsewhere on the Rhine, and just 10.5 million euros to convert three cargo vessels, out of thousands, to shallow-draft, low-emission designs. Tooze's own sourcing calls that "a gesture rather than the beginning of a fleet renewal."
SAM: Does this connect to anything we've been tracking on this side of the Atlantic?
ALEX: The mechanism's identical to what we've flagged domestically — a Colorado easement stranding a gigawatt of finished wind, four-year transformer lead times, Texas's own forecaster warning large loads won't have power by 2030. Physical and political bottlenecks binding faster than capital can move, just showing up in European freight logistics instead of American power.
SAM: What's the honest caveat here?
ALEX: That the "easy" fix — dredging — genuinely does solve the local number. Barges move again, the headline problem looks solved. It's the systemic cost, pushed downstream in time and geography, that doesn't show up until the next dry year. That's a pattern worth watching for anywhere a quick fix and a structural fix are in tension — energy included.
