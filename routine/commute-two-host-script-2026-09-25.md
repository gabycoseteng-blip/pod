# The Morning Commute — Two-Host Script (LONG / 1.5x edition)
### Friday, September 25, 2026

**Voices:** ALEX = Host A, domain expert (Sulafat). SAM = Host B, sharp generalist (Charon).
**Style (render):** Two polished financial-news anchors — crisp, authoritative, controlled energy, British accent.
**Audience assumption:** fluent in markets and current events. No 101 explainers; lead with the number and the second-order read.
**Segment rules:** open straight into the first story — NO run-of-show / segment preview in the cold open. There is NO meme segment. Close on a ~1 min "One Good Thing" + a warm sign-off.

> RENDER NOTE: Only `ALEX:` / `SAM:` lines are spoken. `##` headers and `<!-- … -->` comments are silent. ONE line per turn — no markdown, no line breaks inside a turn. Numbers as NUMERALS.

## SEGMENT ONE — HEADLINES

ALEX: Israeli elections are in October, and this reads as campaign positioning dressed up as foreign policy — picking a fight with a sitting US mayor plays to a domestic audience that wants defiance, not diplomacy, right now. The walkout itself is becoming the story more than anything he actually said.

SAM: Different continent, actual shooting — Ethiopia's Tigray war is back on.

ALEX: Tigrayan forces seized three federal-controlled airports Wednesday — Mekelle, Shire, and Axum — and took federal police guards prisoner. Ethiopian Airlines suspended all three routes immediately. The TPLF also announced a new alliance with six other armed groups aiming to remove the government in Addis Ababa, and fighting's already spread into the neighboring Afar region.

SAM: Wasn't there a peace deal for this exact war?

ALEX: The 2022 African Union-mediated deal that ended the prior two-year civil war — both sides are now accusing each other of violating it. This is a real risk of that war resuming, not a border skirmish.

SAM: What would actually resuming look like, practically?

ALEX: Federal forces have to decide whether to try retaking three airports by force, which is a real war, or negotiate again from a weaker position than 2022 — the TPLF didn't just seize infrastructure, it built a seven-group coalition explicitly aimed at regime change, which is a materially bigger ask than the last round of fighting. Addis Ababa hasn't said which path it's taking yet. US Business.

## SEGMENT ONE — U.S. BUSINESS

SAM: Space M&A first — Rocket Lab's Iridium deal just cleared its last hurdle.

ALEX: Iridium shareholders approved the acquisition today, 99.6% of votes cast in favor. $27 a share cash plus Rocket Lab stock, roughly $8 billion total, closing expected mid-2027. It's one of the largest space-sector deals of the year.

SAM: And the housing data that dropped yesterday is more complicated than the headline.

ALEX: August new-home sales jumped 6.4% to 684,000 annualized, the fastest pace of 2026 — but the median new-home price fell 5.8% year-over-year to $393,700. Builders are buying that volume with discounts, not demand.

SAM: How widespread is the discounting?

ALEX: There's 8.5 months of supply at the current sales pace, and Parcl Labs says roughly 42% of all active US listings now carry a price cut — a record. Read the volume number as builders clearing inventory at a lower clearing price, not the housing market actually strengthening.

SAM: Does that change how you'd read the next Fed data point?

ALEX: It should — a volume beat built on record price-cutting is disinflationary at the household level even while the headline print looks like resilient demand. Anyone reading August's sales number as a strength signal without checking the price line is going to misread the consumer entirely. International.

ALEX: Rodríguez promises elections with no date, Lecornu's budget might not survive its own parliament, and we'll get to a third one when we hit China.

SAM: Is the France number actually a France problem, or a broader signal?

ALEX: Watch it as a template — a G7 economy with no majority government and a debt load past 119% of GDP is a preview of how bond markets price political paralysis in any advanced democracy that can't pass a budget. Four-and-a-half percent on a French ten-year would have been unthinkable pre-COVID; now it's a live number with a clear political cause attached, not a mystery move. Markets.

## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

ALEX: This week's Economist flags something worth actually chewing on — the backlash against Meta's camera glasses has a name now: "pervert glasses."

SAM: That's a headline doing a lot of work.

ALEX: It's earned it. Meta's camera-equipped Ray-Bans have become a real flashpoint — men secretly filming and harassing women in public, a Dutch privacy group called Bits of Freedom filming Meta's own employees to make the point stick, London pubs and UK courtrooms banning the glasses outright. There's now a lawsuit alleging human annotators reviewed footage of people undressing, using bathrooms, and handling financial documents that got swept into Meta's AI-training pipeline.

SAM: What's Meta actually doing about it, beyond PR?

ALEX: Two real moves. A mandatory firmware update that disables the camera if the privacy LED is tampered with — closing the "cover the light, keep filming" loophole — and a camera-free glasses line launching at this week's Connect conference. That second move is the tell: they're hedging the category, not just defending the current product.

SAM: Meanwhile Google and Samsung are walking straight into the same fire.

ALEX: Their jointly-developed camera glasses launch this fall regardless — betting the category survives the backlash rather than that it needs the same retreat Meta's making. That's the actual fork worth watching: does "ambient AI hardware," which every lab wants as the post-smartphone form factor, standardize on camera-free by default, or does the market just route around the backlash the way Instagram's been playing whack-a-mole with banned accounts?

SAM: Which way does the law point?

ALEX: The UK courtroom bans and Wetherspoons' pub ban are consent-law responses arriving faster than any US regulation — that's usually the leading indicator for how a global product category eventually gets designed. If enough jurisdictions treat always-on cameras on strangers as a default privacy violation rather than an opt-out feature, the hardware roadmap bends around that, not around what Silicon Valley wants to ship.

SAM: First real test of whether ambient AI hardware and consent norms can actually coexist.

ALEX: Exactly the stakes. And it's happening while Meta's simultaneously facing a lawsuit over what its own annotators saw — the backlash isn't hypothetical anymore, there's an actual discovery process running underneath the PR response.

SAM: What's the Dutch privacy group's actual point, filming Meta's own staff?

ALEX: Bits of Freedom turned the camera on Meta's own Amsterdam-area employees deliberately — the argument is that the people building the product wouldn't tolerate being on the other end of it, so the demonstration doubles as both protest and evidence. It's a more effective piece of advocacy than a white paper, because it's the exact scenario the company's own defenders keep calling overblown, played out on people who can't opt out of being recognized as employees of the company that made the glasses.

SAM: Does the camera-free line actually solve anything, or is it just a release valve?

ALEX: It's a release valve dressed as a solution — it doesn't touch the installed base of camera-equipped units already in pubs and on trains, and it doesn't resolve the annotator lawsuit. What it does is give Meta a product to point to in every future regulatory hearing: "we offer a privacy-respecting option," even while the flagship product stays exactly as it is. Energy.

## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES

ALEX: Oracle just used a tool nobody's used publicly on an AI campus contract before — force majeure, on its own flagship build.

SAM: Force majeure against its own project?

ALEX: Against its landlord, effectively. Oracle sent a force-majeure notice to Stack Infrastructure, a Blue Owl Capital unit developing Project Jupiter — the Stargate and OpenAI-tied AI campus in Doña Ana County, New Mexico. Total buildout's up to $165 billion, and it's about 26% built.

SAM: What actually triggered it — a generation problem?

ALEX: That's the interesting part — no. The power design is up to 2.45 gigawatts of Bloom Energy solid-oxide fuel cells; Oracle dropped its original gas-turbine plan back in April specifically to dodge the turbine backlog and local opposition. The generation side is fine. The failure point is a single 17-mile gas pipeline feeding those fuel cells.

SAM: What's wrong with the pipeline?

ALEX: The New Mexico State Land Office has repeatedly denied right-of-way across state trust land. The route's been redrawn, and the in-service date has already slipped from August 2026 to February 2027. There's a pending air-quality permit ruling due November 23rd that could force a redesign of the entire power system, not just the pipeline.

SAM: So what does the force-majeure notice actually do for Oracle here?

ALEX: It's not an exit — Oracle isn't walking away as anchor tenant. It's securing the right to delay payments if the campus misses its 2028 open, which shifts the timing and completion risk onto Blue Owl, the landlord, rather than Oracle eating it as offtaker. Oracle shares fell 3 to 5% premarket the same day the notice became public.

SAM: Is this a one-off, or a template?

ALEX: I'd call it the first big public instance of a hyperscaler reaching for force-majeure optionality on an AI-campus contract specifically — worth watching as a template other offtakers copy as pipeline, interconnect, and permitting timelines keep slipping against 2027-28 in-service commitments across the board. It's also the cleanest example yet of the "de-risk generation, get stuck on gas transport" pattern — fuel cells solved the turbine-scarcity risk, they didn't solve the right-of-way risk underneath them.

SAM: Different chokepoint, same theme — what's the DOE done on transmission this week?

ALEX: DOE announced $1.9 billion in federal funding yesterday for 31 grid-reconductoring projects across 26 states under its SPARK program, expected to add 23 gigawatts of transmission capacity — total project investment $5.25 billion once you add the $3.35 billion in recipient cost-share. Scope is reconductoring or replacing over 1,500 miles of existing lines, plus grid-enhancing tech across roughly 21,000 miles.

SAM: Why reconductor instead of building new lines?

ALEX: Because it's the politically and permitting-cheap way to add gigawatt-scale capacity — you're upgrading conductors on existing right-of-way instead of fighting a multi-year new-line siting battle. DOE's own language is explicit about this being faster than greenfield transmission. Put next to Oracle's pipeline problem, it's the same underlying pattern: the last mile of energy delivery — gas transport in Oracle's case, electric transmission here — is where AI-infrastructure timelines actually break, not the generation source itself. ## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

ALEX: Today's first word is 搁置, gēzhì — to shelve or set aside an issue, often indefinitely and deliberately.

SAM: How's that different from just delaying something?

ALEX: That's exactly the contrast — 拖延, tuōyán, means to delay or procrastinate, and it carries a whiff of avoidance or inefficiency, usually personal. 搁置 is institutional and deliberate — a conscious decision to set something aside, often for an indefinite period, with no implied criticism attached. It's formal, written-register — 书面语 — standard in diplomatic and policy reporting. Common collocations: 搁置争议, shelve a dispute; 搁置计划, shelve a plan; 被搁置的议题, an issue that's been shelved.

SAM: Give us the example we'll hear in a minute.

ALEX: 台湾问题和芯片管制这次被双方搁置，没有列入公报。 The Taiwan issue and chip controls were shelved by both sides this time, and weren't included in the communiqué.

SAM: Second word — 一旦, followed by 就.

ALEX: 一旦…就 — "once… then." It's an advanced HSK-4 connective marking a single triggering event after which a consequence follows, often irreversibly. Contrast it with 如果…就, rúguǒ…jiù, "if… then," which is a neutral hypothetical that may or may not happen. 一旦…就 implies the trigger is a real, often sudden turning point — heavier stakes once it occurs.

SAM: Example.

ALEX: 一旦台湾问题被重新提起，中美关系就可能急转直下。 Once the Taiwan issue is raised again, US-China relations could take a sharp turn for the worse. Both words, straight into the China story.

ALEX: Because neither side had anything to gain from forcing the harder conversation this week — both needed a deliverable, and trade was the achievable one. 搁置, not resolved. ALEX: Beijing benefits more, on balance — every month Taiwan and AI security stay 搁置 is a month China doesn't have to make a costly concession on either, while the trade track keeps propping up its export engine. Washington gets a calmer news cycle out of the truce; Beijing gets that plus time. It's not a symmetrical shelving, even if it looks like one from the joint statement. Arts.

## SEGMENT TEN — OP-EDS & COMMENTARY

ALEX: Lionel Laurent in Bloomberg Opinion this week — Europe, he argues, is on the menu.

SAM: Steelman it.

ALEX: Laurent's borrowing Mark Carney's line — if you're not at the table, you're on the menu — to argue the Trump-Xi truce gives Xi more room to lean on the EU specifically. ALEX: The "Europe is a powerless middle power" framing is a recurring genre in this exact column — it understates real EU leverage. SAM: Second piece — the Washington Post on the White House press fight.

ALEX: The Post's editorial board argues Trump can't replace the media he's trying to silence. After banning CNN, MS NOW, and Politico from pool access, the administration launched a YouTube livestream called Trump TV as a substitute — it flopped — while a "Media Offenders" page on the official White House site names outlets it says publish inaccuracies.

SAM: How did the press corps actually respond?

ALEX: Major networks halted video coverage of the president in solidarity, the banned outlets sued in federal court, and other broadcasters — including Gray TV — declined the White House's invitation to fill CNN's now-vacant pool seat. SAM: What's the honest counter to that?

ALEX: The Post is a direct party to this fight, not a neutral observer — worth flagging plainly. And most people now get news through podcasts, Substack, and social video anyway, so a White House pool ban may matter far less to actual information flow than it would have a decade ago. This is also a live lawsuit, not a settled outcome — the piece is making a prediction, not reporting a result.

SAM: Does the failed livestream tell you anything about the actual audience appetite here?

ALEX: It tells you state-run alternative media doesn't automatically inherit an audience just because the original channel gets cut off — people didn't migrate to Trump TV, they mostly just watched less of any official feed at all. That's a genuinely inconvenient data point for both sides of this argument: it undercuts the administration's bet that it could route around the press, but it also undercuts the Post's implicit assumption that people are hungry for adversarial coverage specifically, rather than just news in general.
