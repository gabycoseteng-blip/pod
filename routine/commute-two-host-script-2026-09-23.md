## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE
ALEX: Today's Deep Dive comes from The Economist's daily newsletter, and it's not markets or geopolitics — it's labor economics, and it's one of the cleanest natural experiments I've seen in a while.
SAM: Go on.
ALEX: There's a paper in the Review of Economic Studies, using Danish registry data from 1993 to 2017, on owner-managed firms with a single male manager. The identification strategy is beautifully simple — the sex of a manager's first child is essentially a coin flip, so you can treat it as random.
SAM: And?
ALEX: When that first child turns out to be a daughter instead of a son, women's relative earnings at that manager's firm rise about 4.4%, and the female employment share rises about 2.9% — and it happens almost immediately after the birth, not over some slow multi-year drift.
SAM: So having a daughter functionally recalibrates how a boss treats every woman who works for him.
ALEX: That's the argument, and it's a real answer to a question that's usually impossible to settle empirically — is discriminatory behavior a fixed trait you're stuck measuring, or is it a revealed preference that shifts when the facts of someone's life change? This paper says the latter, with numbers attached.
SAM: Which matters enormously for how boards and HR departments think about intervention design. If attitudes are fixed, you regulate outcomes — quotas, mandated pay audits, hard rules. If attitudes are malleable and respond to lived proximity, the more interesting lever might be things like mentorship or job-shadowing programs that manufacture that proximity artificially, instead of waiting for biology to do it.
ALEX: Exactly — it reframes the entire DEI-policy debate away from "is bias real" and toward "what's the cheapest way to simulate having a daughter." The Economist's attribution here is Stephanie Studer's newsletter from Sunday, and the underlying paper's also been picked up by Aarhus Business School and Marginal Revolution, so this isn't a one-outlet curiosity — it's already circulating in the econ-blogosphere as a genuine landmark identification strategy.
SAM: There's a limitation worth naming, though — this is owner-managed, single-manager firms specifically, so it's isolating one man's discretion with unusually little institutional friction around him. A large public company with an HR department, a comp committee, and layers of middle management might show a much smaller version of this effect, or none at all, because the manager's personal attitude has fewer direct levers to pull.
ALEX: That's the honest caveat, but it actually strengthens the policy read rather than weakening it — if a single owner-manager's private epiphany moves the needle 4.4% with zero institutional support, then a company that deliberately engineers that same proximity at scale — sponsorship programs pairing senior men with junior women, cross-functional rotations, whatever the mechanism — should be able to capture more of that effect, not less, precisely because it has the institutional muscle this study's firms lack.
SAM: So the actionable version of this paper isn't "hope your CEO has a daughter" — it's "build the proximity deliberately, since biology just proved the mechanism works."
SAM: Is there a comparable effect if the first child's a son?
ALEX: The paper doesn't find one in either direction — a first son doesn't measurably move female pay or hiring at all. It's specifically the daughter that shifts something, which the authors read as evidence this is about a manager suddenly imagining a woman's career trajectory as personally relevant to him, not some generic "becoming a parent softens you" story.
SAM: Rare for a Deep Dive to leave the AI-capex-and-tariffs lane entirely, and honestly it's a nice change of scenery.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
ALEX: Time for Mandarin vocabulary, and both words today tie into the China story coming up right after this.
SAM: First word: 姑息 — gūxī. It means to indulge, appease, or tolerate something you shouldn't — to let a problem slide rather than confront it. It's formal register, 书面语, the kind of word you'd see in a policy document or financial press, not casual conversation.
ALEX: The near-synonym worth contrasting is 纵容, zòngróng — that one also means to indulge or connive, but it carries a much harsher moral judgment, almost "complicity." 姑息 is softer — closer to "looking the other way" than "conspiring." Example: 监管机构选择姑息这家公司的债务问题。
SAM: "Regulators chose to look the other way on the company's debt problem." Note the collocation — 姑息 pairs naturally with 问题, "problem," and often with 危机, crisis — you're indulging a problem, not a person, in this register.
ALEX: Second word, and this one's a connective: 以免 — yǐmiǎn. It means "so as not to," or "in order to avoid" — you use it to introduce the negative outcome you're trying to prevent.
SAM: Worth flagging the contrast with 以便, yǐbiàn, which we taught yesterday — 以便 sets up a positive goal you're working toward, while 以免 sets up a negative outcome you're working away from. Same grammatical slot, opposite orientation.
ALEX: Example sentence: 银行推迟处理这笔贷款，以免触发系统性风险。
SAM: "Banks delayed handling the loan, so as not to trigger systemic risk." That's exactly the mechanism in today's China story — you'll hear both words again in a moment.
ALEX: 姑息 for the appeasement, 以免 for the avoidance logic behind it — that's your pair for today.
