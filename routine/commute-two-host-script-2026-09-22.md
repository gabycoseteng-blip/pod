## SEGMENT FOUR — ENERGY / DATA CENTERS / UTILITIES
SAM: Energy — and there's a genuinely sharp new argument this week about why clean buildout keeps losing to gas behind the meter.
ALEX: This is David Riester, of Segue Sustainable Infrastructure, in DealFlow.energy's newsletter — a piece called "The Last 10%." His thesis: solar-plus-storage covers about 90% of a large load's annual energy cheaply and fast. Batteries solve the diurnal problem well.

ALEX: Pushing 90 to 95% roughly doubles the plan's cost. 95 to 100 is a further multiple on top of that — because the remaining hours aren't scattered randomly, they're clustered and correlated, a run of overcast January days when the whole system is short at once, not just one site.

ALEX: That's Riester's actual point — there's no standard tariffed product for it. SAM: Which means the gas plant ends up the same size as the data center.
ALEX: Same nameplate capacity — roughly ten times what the actual energy gap required — running at a capacity factor that would embarrass a peaker plant, and contributing nothing to anyone else's reliability because it's fenced off behind that one meter. SAM: What's his actual fix?

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB
SAM: Two Mandarin words today, both built directly for the China story coming up — a new mechanism, and the reason for building it.
ALEX: First word: 机制, jīzhì — "mechanism." An abstract noun, formal register, used constantly in policy and diplomacy writing for a structured process or system, not a physical device.
SAM: What does it typically pair with?
ALEX: 建立机制, "to establish a mechanism," or 通知机制, "a notification mechanism" — which is exactly the phrase in today's story. It's distinct from 制度, which leans toward a formal institution or system of rules; 机制 is more about the actual working process underneath.
ALEX: Example: 双方同意建立一个新的通知机制，以应对未来的危机。("Both sides agreed to establish a new notification mechanism to respond to future crises.")
SAM: Second word — 以便, yǐbiàn.
ALEX: An HSK-4 connective meaning "so that" or "in order that" — it introduces the purpose or intended result of an action just described, and it's more formal than the everyday 为了, which usually comes before the action instead of after it.
SAM: How does the word order actually work?
ALEX: Action first, then 以便, then the purpose — the opposite structure from 为了, which usually opens the sentence. 以便 signals "here's the outcome we're setting up for," almost like "in order to enable."
ALEX: Example: 两国官员提前交换信息，以便在危机发生前采取行动。("Officials from both countries are exchanging information in advance, so that they can act before a crisis happens.") We'll hear both of these again in a moment.
