## SEGMENT THREE — DEEP DIVE

SAM: Our deep dive this morning is off the usual beat. It's a piece of public health science that has been going around this week, and it's about how much risk sits in an ordinary evening drink. The paper is by George and colleagues, with Kevin Shield of the University of Toronto as lead author, in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, called the Alcohol Intake and Health Study. ScienceDaily and Forbes covered it again this week, and I'll flag that I'm working from the abstract and press coverage, not the full paper.
ALEX: The headline finding is a lifetime figure. At an average of 14 drinks a week, so 2 a day, the study puts the risk of dying from an alcohol-caused disease or injury at about 1 in 25. At up to 7 drinks a week, the risks are only minimally elevated for most conditions. And the authors say they found no significant protective effect at any level overall, which is the part that overturns a long-running assumption.
SAM: Right, and the method is what makes it unusual. They reviewed more than 7,200 articles on alcohol-related disease, applied those relative risks to national health datasets, and modeled lifetime outcomes. The result is the authors recommending no more than 1 drink a day for current adult drinkers. Forbes, quoting a physician, puts 1 drink a day at roughly 1 in 1,000 for lifetime death risk.
ALEX: Now the backdrop, which is why this got attention. The study was commissioned by the US federal government to inform the new Dietary Guidelines, per the UCL summary of the abstract. So it isn't a random cohort paper, it's an input into policy on what Americans are told is safe. That policy fight, over alcohol and cancer labeling and over what counts as moderate, is live.
SAM: Let's do the skeptic's side seriously, because this is a place where the framing matters. The first issue is the old one in alcohol epidemiology. The classic J-shaped curve, where light drinkers seem healthier than abstainers, was blamed for years on the sick-quitter problem, former drinkers who stopped because they got ill, who contaminate the abstainer group. Modern studies correct for that, and this one reports no net protective effect. Though the authors do note possible small reductions in ischemic heart disease and stroke at low levels, outweighed by cancer and everything else.
ALEX: The second issue is observational confounding. Nearly all of the underlying evidence is observational, so the risk curves lean on how well each study adjusted for income, diet and smoking. That doesn't kill the finding, but it's why the confidence intervals matter more than the headline.
SAM: The third is the framing, and I think it's the most important one for listeners. A lifetime risk of 1 in 25 sounds frightening, but it is a population average. Shield himself cautions that an individual's risk depends on genetics, lifestyle and drinking patterns. And there's a difference between relative and absolute risk, because a 1 in 25 lifetime figure is spread over decades, the annual hazard is tiny, and the lifetime number is dominated by the heaviest, longest-term drinkers.
ALEX: Also, reports differ on whether the numbers are sex-specific. Some coverage says the 1 in 25 figure is about men, others say men and women are similar, so I'd treat that detail as unsettled until the full tables are read.
SAM: So what should we take from it? To me the point is less the number than the direction. For 30 years the message was that moderation is fine, maybe good. The strongest current synthesis says there's no free lunch, with risk rising smoothly from the first drink, though from a low base.
ALEX: And the second-order angle is commercial. Spirits and beer companies have spent heavily defending moderate drinking as a lifestyle category, and the global trade in spirits is a tariff target in the same trade fights we cover elsewhere. If guidelines tighten to 1 drink a day for everyone, the category narrative moves from a harmless pleasure to a managed risk, like sugar a decade ago.
SAM: The honest way to hold it is that the evidence got a bit sharper and the advice got more conservative. It doesn't say a glass of wine is a hazard. It says the old reassurance wasn't earned, and that the case for the second drink is weaker than we told ourselves.
ALEX: Pour accordingly.

## SEGMENT SEVEN — MANDARIN VOCAB

SAM: Two words today, both from China's profit data. The first is 盈利, yínglì. It means profit, or to turn a profit, and it works as both a noun and a verb.
ALEX: The contrast to know is with 利润, lìrùn. 利润 is the profit figure, the line on a statement. 盈利 is the state or act of making money, so you'd say a company 开始盈利, begins to be profitable, and the opposite is 亏损, kuīsǔn, a loss. It's formal and common in business writing.
SAM: Here's an example: 尽管消费不振，电子行业的盈利还是大幅增长。(Even though consumption is weak, profits in the electronics industry still grew sharply.) Notice the 尽管 and 还是 pair, which is exactly the contrast structure we've been drilling.
ALEX: The second is a connective, and a more abstract one: 与其说……不如说……, yǔqí shuō, bùrú shuō. It means rather than say X, it's better to say Y.
SAM: And it's a reframing tool. It corrects a label rather than choosing between actions. Contrast 与其……不如……, which weighs two courses of action, as in rather than wait, better to act. The 说 version argues about how something should be described.
ALEX: It's slightly formal, the sort of phrase a columnist uses. Example: 与其说这是复苏，不如说这是少数行业的繁荣。(Rather than call this a recovery, it's better to call it a boom in a few sectors.)
SAM: Both come back in the China segment next, so listen for them.
